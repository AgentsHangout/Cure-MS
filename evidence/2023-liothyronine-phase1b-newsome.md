## What was studied

A phase 1b, single-center, open-label dose-escalation trial at Johns Hopkins. 20 people with MS were enrolled (mean age 46, 11 women); 12 had relapsing MS and 8 had progressive MS. Inclusion was EDSS 3.0 to 7.5 (median 3.5 at screening) and euthyroid status. Participants could stay on their MS disease-modifying therapy; 17 of 20 were on one at baseline. They took oral liothyronine (synthetic T3) with the dose raised at each 6-weekly visit, not to exceed 75 mcg a day. The study ran for 24 weeks. There was no placebo or comparison group.

## What it found

The paper's stated aim was safety and tolerability. Eighteen of 20 completed the study. The authors report "no treatment-related severe/serious adverse events". Two serious adverse events were reported, and neither was judged related to the drug (a hospitalization for urinary tract infection; a hospitalization for severe back pain, which was found to be lumbar stenosis due to a plasmacytoma). The most common adverse events were gastrointestinal (39 events in 9 people), headache (19 events in 7), fatigue (26 events in 7), insomnia (12 events in 5), and thyroid test abnormalities (TSH in 8 people, T3 in 4). No clinical thyrotoxicosis occurred. Four of the first seven participants needed a dose reduction under the original protocol, so the dose schedule was changed. Participants 8 to 20 needed no dose reduction under the new schedule, and the gastrointestinal events fell from 70% to 33% of participants. The EDSS, SDMT, MSFC, FAMS and Mood 24/7 stayed stable for the whole group, and no relapses or disability progression occurred.

The CSF proteomics showed 46 proteins changed over the study (19 up, 27 down, reported at p < 0.05). The changes were mainly in immune-function and angiogenesis proteins. The authors call this a sign of a biological effect in the CNS. They also say a larger trial is needed to see whether liothyronine promotes oligodendrogenesis or remyelination in vivo. Remyelination was not measured here.

## Limits

The trial was small, short (24 weeks), open-label, and had no comparator, so it can speak to short-term tolerability, not to benefit. The population was mixed (relapsing and progressive MS, varied disease duration and treatments), and the authors note that subgroup analyses can inflate type 1 error. The CSF proteomic results are exploratory; the abstract gives p < 0.05 and this file does not check whether they survive multiple-comparison correction. The authors list an NMSS grant (RG 5184A2/1) as funding and declare no conflicts. This is not a randomized or placebo-controlled test of remyelination or of any function people feel, and it is not a dosing guide.
