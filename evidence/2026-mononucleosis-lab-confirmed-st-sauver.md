# Laboratory-confirmed EBV mononucleosis and later MS in Minnesota

- **Citation:** St Sauver JL, Hall SA, Jacobson RM, et al. Risk of Multiple Sclerosis Among Persons with Epstein Barr Virus-Positive Mononucleosis: A Population-Based Study. *Neurology Open Access*, 2026;2(2):e000082.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1212/wn9.0000000000000082](https://doi.org/10.1212/wn9.0000000000000082), PMID [42022360](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42022360/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (retrospective population-based cohort study)
- **Question:** [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

Many earlier studies did not confirm that IM was caused by EBV, or found MS cases from billing codes.
This study used medical records from the Rochester Epidemiology Project (Minnesota, US). It compared
4,721 people with blood-test evidence of EBV infection and an IM diagnosis between 1998 and 2022 (55%
female, 70% under 20) with 14,163 people of the same age and sex without IM (3 for each exposed
person). New MS cases were confirmed by experts reviewing charts without knowing who had IM.

## What it found

- People with EBV-positive IM were more likely to develop MS during follow-up: 8 of 4,721 (0.17%)
  vs 10 of 14,163 (0.07%) in the comparison group.
- Adjusted hazard ratio 3.14 (95% CI 1.18 to 8.34).
- Median follow-up was 6 years for the IM group and 8 years for the comparison group.

## Limits

- Only 18 MS cases in total, so the estimate is imprecise (wide confidence interval).
- Observational, so it shows association, not cause. The comparison group was people without IM,
  not people without EBV; many of them will have had EBV infection without IM.
- Follow-up is fairly short for an illness that often starts years after IM.
- One region of the US.
- The abstract lists some author affiliations that it does not name; check the full paper for
  funding and any industry links.
