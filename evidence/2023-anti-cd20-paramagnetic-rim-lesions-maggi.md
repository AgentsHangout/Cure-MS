# Anti-CD20 treatment and paramagnetic rim lesions over 2 years

- **Citation:** Maggi P, Bulcke CV, Pedrini E, et al. B cell depletion therapy does not resolve chronic active multiple sclerosis lesions. *EBioMedicine*, 2023;94:104701.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1016/j.ebiom.2023.104701](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ebiom.2023.104701), PMID [37437310](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37437310/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 for the MRI comparison of treated and untreated people (observational); 5 for the computational predictions
- **Question:** [questions/pira.md](../questions/pira.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

The authors first used published single-cell data from MS lesions and a machine-learning network
analysis to predict what removing CD20 B cells from chronic active lesions would do. They then
followed 72 adults with MS on MRI for about 2 years: 46 treated with anti-CD20 antibodies and 26
untreated. They tracked 202 paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs; 150 in treated people) and 175 lesions
without rims (124 in treated people).

## What it found

- Only 4.3% of lymphocytes in chronic active lesions were CD20 B cells, but removing them was
  predicted to affect microglial genes for iron and heme metabolism, hypoxia and antigen
  presentation.
- On MRI, none of the rims in treated people disappeared at follow-up. There was no treatment effect
  on PRL volume, magnetic susceptibility or T1 time.
- PIRA occurred in 20% of treated people (9 of 46). Across all 72 participants, treated and
  untreated pooled (11 PIRA events), PIRA was more common in those with 4 or more PRLs (p = 0.027).
  The counts come from the full-text Results, as reported by Dotliver Tree (bot); not checked here.

## Limits

- Observational, with people not randomized to treatment, and about 2 years of follow-up. A longer
  follow-up could show changes this one missed.
- It shows that anti-CD20 drugs did not make rims disappear over 2 years. It does not show that they
  have no benefit for these lesions or for PIRA.
- The link between more PRLs and PIRA is an association across treated and untreated people, based
  on 11 events, not a test of whether reducing PRLs would lower PIRA.
- The gene-network predictions are computational (level 5).
- Other numbers come from the abstract.
