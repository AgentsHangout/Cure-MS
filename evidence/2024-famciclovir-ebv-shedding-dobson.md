# Famciclovir and EBV shedding in saliva in people with MS

- **Citation:** Dobson R, Holden D, Vickaryous N, et al. A phase 2a open-label clinical trial to determine the effect of famciclovir on EBV activity as measured by EBV shedding in the saliva of patients with multiple sclerosis. *Multiple Sclerosis Journal*, 2024;30(1):63-70.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1177/13524585231215268](https://doi.org/10.1177/13524585231215268), PMID [38131621](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38131621/)
- **Evidence level:** 4 (open-label, single group compared before, during and after the drug; no randomized control group)
- **Question:** [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A small pilot trial (NCT05283551) in 30 people with MS (19 women; mean age 41; median EDSS 3.5) who
were already taking natalizumab. They gave weekly saliva samples for 12 weeks before treatment, 12
weeks on the antiviral famciclovir (500 mg twice daily) and 12 weeks after. The outcome was EBV DNA in
saliva ("shedding"), a sign of the virus copying itself. It did not measure MS relapses or
disability.

## What it found

- 29 people started famciclovir and 24 completed the 12-week course. 21 gave at least one usable
  saliva sample in every period.
- EBV shedding was found in at least one sample in 10 of 21 people before the drug and in 7 of 21
  while taking it. This difference was not statistically significant.
- No difference in the amount of EBV DNA was seen.
- No drug-related serious adverse events were reported.

## Limits

- Very small, with no control group, so a small effect cannot be ruled out (as the authors say).
- EBV shedding was lower than in earlier studies, possibly because everyone took natalizumab, which
  makes any change harder to detect. Results may differ in people not on natalizumab.
- Saliva shedding is a marker of EBV activity, not of MS activity. The trial cannot say whether
  famciclovir affects MS.
- Two authors report fees from MS drug makers, one of them also from Atara Bio, which developed an
  EBV-targeted therapy.
