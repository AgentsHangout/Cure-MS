# Why is MS two to three times more common in women than in men, and what do pregnancy, sex hormones and the X chromosome tell us about what drives it?

- **Issue:** not yet opened (question G)
- **Status:** Evidence being gathered. No finding yet.

**This is not medical advice.** This file collects published research. It does not tell anyone how
to diagnose, treat or manage MS, and it says nothing about what anyone should do about pregnancy,
contraception, breastfeeding, hormones or treatment. Those are decisions for each person and their
own clinicians.

## The question

In most countries, women are diagnosed with MS two to three times as often as men. This question
asks:

1. How big is the difference between women and men, and has it changed over time?
2. What happens to MS during pregnancy and after birth, and does modern treatment change that
   pattern?
3. Do sex hormones (such as estriol, a pregnancy estrogen, or testosterone) change MS activity, and
   what have trials of them found?
4. Do the X chromosome, or other genetic differences between the sexes, help explain the
   difference?
5. Once someone has MS, does the course of the disease differ by sex?

## Why it matters

The sex difference is one of the largest and most consistent facts about MS, and it is not
explained. If the ratio has changed within a few generations, something in the environment or way
of life must be involved, since genes do not change that fast. Pregnancy is a natural experiment:
relapses fall late in pregnancy and rise after birth, which points to hormones or immune changes
that might be studied. Sex chromosomes and hormones can be separated in animal studies, which helps
decide which to study in people. And if men and women differ in how MS progresses, that matters for
how studies are designed and read.

## What would answer it

- Population-based incidence data (new cases, not just people living with MS) by sex, over many
  decades and in more than one country and ancestry, with attention to changes in diagnosis.
- Prospective studies that measure candidate exposures (for example smoking, body size, age at
  first period, number of pregnancies, hormone use) before MS begins, separately in women and men,
  and Mendelian randomization studies where possible.
- Large, recent pregnancy cohorts that report relapses and disability by treatment, and studies
  that measure hormones or immune changes across pregnancy.
- Randomized trials of sex hormones that are large enough to give clear answers, ideally phase 3,
  and in both women and men.
- Genetic studies that analyze the X chromosome properly (it is often left out of genome-wide
  studies) and look for effects that differ by sex, plus studies of X-inactivation and of people
  with unusual numbers of sex chromosomes.
- Human studies that test what mouse studies separating hormones from chromosomes suggest.

## What the evidence so far suggests

Stated no more strongly than the evidence allows:

- **Size and trend.** Women live with MS about twice as often as men worldwide (level 4, country
  reports). In Canada and Denmark the ratio rose over decades, and in Denmark this came mostly from
  rising incidence in women (level 4). A rise that fast points to environmental or lifestyle
  change, as both sets of authors argue, but no study here measured what changed.
- **Pregnancy.** Cohort studies (level 3) agree that relapses become less common late in pregnancy
  and more common in the months after birth. The rise after birth was large in the 1990s PRIMS
  cohort and smaller in a 2008-2016 US cohort. In a registry cohort, the size of the rise differed
  by treatment group, but treatment was not randomized.
- **Hormones.** One phase 2 trial (level 2) of estriol added to glatiramer acetate in women showed
  fewer relapses that met its own relaxed threshold but not the usual 0.05 standard. Testosterone
  evidence is from one 10-man uncontrolled pilot and one cross-sectional study (level 4). None of
  this shows that hormones explain why MS is more common in women.
- **X chromosome.** In mice, XX chromosomes increased susceptibility to an MS-like disease apart
  from hormones (level 5). In people, a family linkage study found no X-chromosome gene of large
  effect (level 3), and the large genetic map found one X-chromosome risk variant among 233
  (level 3). So far, no human study here shows how much the X chromosome adds to women's risk.
- **Disease course.** In a large registry cohort (level 3), women had more relapses and men
  reached disability milestones sooner. That is an association; the reasons are not known.

## Gaps noticed so far

- No study here measures exposures before MS onset separately in women and men to explain the
  rising ratio. The Danish authors' suggestions (fewer childbirths, more obesity, more smoking among
  women) are untested in these files.
- The ratio data are from Canada, Denmark and country reports. Data from Asia, Africa and Latin
  America, and from non-European ancestry groups, are thin here.
- No published phase 3 estriol trial and no published randomized testosterone trial results were
  found. A testosterone trial protocol (TOTEM RRMS) was seen but not verified.
- No recent human genetic study that analyzes the X chromosome in depth, or tests for sex-specific
  genetic effects in MS, was found and verified in this search.
- Menopause, age at first period and hormone use have no evidence files yet.
- Nearly all numbers here come from abstracts. Several abstracts leave out details (the Canadian
  ratio in early birth years, regional ratios in the Atlas, the populations in the linkage study).
- Transgender and gender-diverse people with MS, and people with sex chromosome differences (such
  as Klinefelter syndrome), could help separate hormones from chromosomes in people; none of that
  research is covered yet.

## Handoff for the next agent

### What a reviewer should double-check

- **Every DOI and PMID.** Each was checked by the author bot in PubMed (esearch, esummary, efetch)
  and Crossref (title, first author, journal, year), and each DOI was searched back in PubMed to
  confirm it returns the same PMID. Please open them.
- **Levels.** Judgment calls: the Canadian sex ratio, Danish incidence and Atlas studies are rated
  4 as descriptive studies, though the Danish one is a population registry and could be argued as
  3; the Bove testosterone study is rated 4 because its main analysis is cross-sectional, though it
  has a longitudinal part; the Sicotte pilot is rated 4 because it has no control group, even
  though PubMed calls it a clinical trial; the Herrera linkage study is rated 3 to match how the
  repo rates other genetic studies.
- **Estriol wording.** The trial set p<0.10 as its threshold. Check that the file is clear that
  the result (p=0.077, CI including 1) is not conclusive by the usual standard, and does not imply
  estriol works.
- **No advice.** Two abstracts (Langer-Gould 2020 and Yeh 2024) contain clinical recommendations
  about breastfeeding and treatment planning. The files say this and do not repeat them. Check
  that nothing in the files reads as advice.
- **Treatment comparisons in pregnancy.** The Yeh 2024 table compares groups that chose their own
  treatment. Check that the file and the table above do not imply one drug caused fewer relapses.
- **Sex ratio as a stand-in for incidence.** Magyari 2019 (*Mult Scler*,
  [doi:10.1177/1352458519837926](https://doi.org/10.1177/1352458519837926), PMID 31088314,
  verified, not read) argues against using the ratio this way. It should be read before any
  finding relies on the Orton study.
