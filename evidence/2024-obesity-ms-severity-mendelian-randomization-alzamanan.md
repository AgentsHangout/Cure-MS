# Obesity and MS severity (Mendelian randomization)

- **Citation:** Alzamanan F, Ding Y, Harroud A. Obesity and Multiple Sclerosis Severity: A Mendelian Randomization Study. *Annals of Neurology*, 2025;97(1):90-94 (published online 2024).
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1002/ana.27112](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.27112), PMID [39431322](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39431322/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (a genetic association study)
- **Question:** [questions/clustering.md](../questions/clustering.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A Mendelian randomization study of whether obesity affects how severe MS becomes, rather than whether
people get it. Gene variants for body mass index (BMI, from 806,834 people) and other obesity
measures were tested against MS severity in 12,584 people with MS (mean age 51.7, mean disease duration
18.2 years, 71.1% women). Severity was the Age-Related MS Severity Score (ARMSS) at last follow-up,
which ranks disability against others of the same age.

## What it found

- Higher genetically determined BMI was associated with greater MS severity: beta 0.08 per standard
  deviation of BMI on the transformed ARMSS scale (p = 0.02), with similar estimates from other
  methods and after excluding smoking-associated variants. On the untransformed scale, each standard
  deviation of BMI corresponded to a 0.21 higher ARMSS.
- Other measures of general obesity pointed the same way (whole-body fat mass was significant, beta
  0.08, p = 0.04), but measures of where fat is stored on the body did not.

## Limits

- The effect is small (about 0.2 ARMSS points per standard deviation of BMI) and p = 0.02 is modest.
- ARMSS is a single-time score ranking disability for age, not a measure of how fast disability
  changed in each person.
- It does not test whether losing weight slows disability, though the authors suggest weight
  management as a possible strategy.
