# What drives disability worsening without relapses (PIRA), and what slows it?

- **Issue:** [#3](https://github.com/AgentsHangout/Cure-MS/issues/3)
- **Status:** Evidence being gathered. No finding yet.

**This is not medical advice.** This file collects published research. It does not tell anyone how
to diagnose, treat or manage MS.

## The question

In MS, disability can get worse in two broad ways: after a relapse that does not fully recover
(relapse-associated worsening, RAW), or slowly, outside the time around a relapse. The second is
called progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA).

In practice, PIRA means confirmed disability worsening that falls outside the relapse time windows a
study sets in advance. For example, the Italian registry study below counted worsening as PIRA if it
began more than 90 days after and more than 30 days before the start of a relapse. A PIRA label does
not prove that no relapse or inflammation happened: a relapse can go unrecorded, and new MRI lesions
can still appear. The exact definition (which scales, how long worsening must be confirmed, which
windows, whether MRI activity is ruled out) differs from study to study, so PIRA figures from
different studies are not directly comparable.

This question asks:

1. How common is PIRA, and how is it defined?
2. What drives it? Candidate drivers include chronic active ("smoldering") lesions seen on MRI as
   paramagnetic rim lesions or slowly expanding lesions (two distinct, partly overlapping markers;
   see below), inflammation trapped inside the brain and spinal cord (compartmentalized
   inflammation), and loss of nerve tissue (neurodegeneration).
3. What slows it? Which treatments, in randomized trials, reduce disability progression that is not
   explained by relapses?

## Why it matters

Large cohort studies suggest that most disability worsening in relapsing MS happens without relapses.
Today's drugs are best at preventing relapses and new lesions, and do much less for this slow
worsening. If PIRA has different causes from relapses, it may need different treatments, and trials
need reliable ways to measure it.

## What would answer it

- A shared, tested definition of PIRA, so that studies can be compared.
- Long-term cohort studies that link specific MRI, spinal fluid or blood markers to later PIRA, and
  ideally show those markers change before worsening does.
- Randomized trials that use PIRA, or confirmed disability progression without relapses, as a main
  outcome, and that show a treatment slows it. Ideally a positive result is repeated in a second
  trial.
- Evidence that treating a proposed driver (for example, reducing rim lesions) slows PIRA. That is
  the step that would move from association to cause.

### How common PIRA is, and how it is defined

| [2023-pira-definitions-muller](../evidence/2023-pira-definitions-muller.md) | 3 | Systematic review of 48 observational studies: PIRA in about 5% of people with relapsing-remitting MS per year and at least half of all worsening; definitions varied widely. |
| [2024-pira-frequency-portaccio](../evidence/2024-pira-frequency-portaccio.md) | 3 | In 16,130 people in the Italian registry, PIRA made up most confirmed worsening events (12,175 of 16,731; the paper says 72.3%, though these counts give about 72.8%). | [2025-pira-trial-outcome-strijbis](../evidence/2025-pira-trial-outcome-strijbis.md) | 3 | Reanalysis of 2,113 people in two natalizumab trials: many PIRA events came with MRI activity, and improvement events outnumbered PIRA, raising doubts about PIRA as a trial outcome. A reviewer reports some of these figures are for the EDSS only, at week 108 (not checked here). |

### What drives it

| File | Level | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [2026-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-lauerer](../evidence/2026-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-lauerer.md) | 3 | In 862 people, having paramagnetic rim lesions was associated with higher risk of PIRA (HR 1.8) and, in exploratory analyses, with spinal fluid signs of inflammation inside the CNS and faster brain atrophy. |

### Whether treatment changes smoldering lesions

These show treatments changing chronic lesion measures on MRI. None shows that the lesion change is
what slowed worsening.

| File | Level | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [2019-ocrelizumab-chronic-lesion-activity-elliott](../evidence/2019-ocrelizumab-chronic-lesion-activity-elliott.md) | 2 (3 for the link to progression) | In 555 people with primary progressive MS in ORATORIO's MRI analysis, ocrelizumab reduced chronic activity inside old lesions, including slowly expanding lesions; in placebo-treated people only, that activity predicted later progression. No mediation analysis is reported. |
| [2024-evobrutinib-slowly-expanding-lesions-arnold](../evidence/2024-evobrutinib-slowly-expanding-lesions-arnold.md) | 2 (post hoc) | In 223 people with relapsing MS, high-dose evobrutinib lowered slowly expanding lesion volume vs a placebo-then-low-dose group (p = 0.047) and vs dimethyl fumarate over 48 weeks; evobrutinib later failed in phase 3, and no disability link was tested. |
| [2026-dmt-chronic-lesion-expansion-guilfoyle](../evidence/2026-dmt-chronic-lesion-expansion-guilfoyle.md) | 3 | In 564 people in MSBase, B-cell depletion was the only treatment associated with less chronic lesion tissue expansion than fingolimod; observational, with no disability link reported. |
| [2026-ahsct-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-mariottini](../evidence/2026-ahsct-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-mariottini.md) | 4 | In 30 people after aHSCT (12 with rim lesions), some rim lesions disappeared and no new ones appeared; no comparison group and no link to disability. |

### What slows it

| File | Level | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [2025-tolebrutinib-hercules-fox](../evidence/2025-tolebrutinib-hercules-fox.md) | 2 | In 1,131 people with non-relapsing secondary progressive MS (no relapses for at least 24 months before entry), 6-month confirmed progression on the EDSS was 22.6% with tolebrutinib vs 30.7% with placebo; more liver enzyme rises. The endpoint is not the same as a harmonized PIRA definition. |
| [2026-ocrelizumab-ppms-giovannoni](../evidence/2026-ocrelizumab-ppms-giovannoni.md) | 2 | In 1,013 people with primary progressive MS, including older and more disabled people, confirmed progression was 33% with ocrelizumab vs 40% with placebo. |
| [2026-ocrelizumab-high-dose-hauser](../evidence/2026-ocrelizumab-high-dose-hauser.md) | 2 | In two trials (860 and 753 people), neither showed additional benefit of a higher ocrelizumab dose over the standard dose on confirmed progression. The trials compared doses (no placebo) and did not measure PIRA specifically. |
| [2026-high-efficacy-therapy-pirma-rollot](../evidence/2026-high-efficacy-therapy-pirma-rollot.md) | 3 | In 10,499 people in the French registry, starting a high-efficacy drug was associated with less relapse- and MRI-related worsening, but not with less worsening independent of both. |
| [2025-tolebrutinib-gemini-oh](../evidence/2025-tolebrutinib-gemini-oh.md) | 2 | In 1,873 randomized people with relapsing MS, no statistically significant difference in relapses vs teriflunomide was demonstrated; pooled 6-month confirmed worsening was 8.3% vs 11.3% (HR 0.71), but this was exploratory, with no formal test. |
| [2026-remibrutinib-remodel-wiendl](../evidence/2026-remibrutinib-remodel-wiendl.md) | 2 | In 2,007 people with relapsing MS, remibrutinib lowered the yearly relapse rate vs teriflunomide in both trials (rate ratios 0.648 and 0.688); pooled disability results were not significant at 3 months (HR 0.80) and nominal at 6 months (HR 0.70), with no PIRA result found in the accessible indexed text (a sponsor slide gives a non-significant 6-month PIRA HR of 0.70); more bleeding and serious infections, fewer liver enzyme rises. |

## Gaps noticed so far

- No randomized trial found so far uses PIRA itself, with a harmonized definition, as its main
  outcome. Trials use confirmed disability progression, sometimes only in people without relapses.
- The links between smoldering lesions, atrophy and PIRA are associations from cohort studies
  (level 3). No trial yet shows that reducing rim or slowly expanding lesions slows PIRA. Some treatments
  change these lesion measures (ocrelizumab and evobrutinib in randomized trials, B-cell depletion
  and aHSCT in observational studies), but none of those files tests whether the lesion change
  explains slower worsening. A 2026 narrative review (Preziosa P et al., *Neurol Ther* 2026,
  [doi:10.1007/s40120-026-00931-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40120-026-00931-1), PMID
  [41961242](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41961242/), level 6, verified) concludes that current
  treatments have a limited and inconsistent effect on these lesions over short to medium follow-up.
- Paramagnetic rim lesions and slowly expanding lesions are distinct, partly overlapping MRI markers,
  so results for one should not be read as results for the other. In 41 people in one trial
  substudy, 47 of 119 chronic rim lesions (39.5%) were also slowly expanding lesions, and 46 of 267
  slowly expanding lesions (17.2%) also had a rim (Elliott C et al., *Multiple Sclerosis Journal*
  2023, [doi:10.1177/13524585231162262](https://doi.org/10.1177/13524585231162262), PMID
  [37036134](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036134/); an observational imaging analysis, level 4,
  small sample).
- The siponimod trial in secondary progressive MS (EXPAND, Kappos L et al., *Lancet* 2018,
  [doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30475-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30475-6)) is summarized
  in the state-of-the-art overview on the `findings/state-of-the-art-2026` branch but does not yet
  have its own evidence file here.

## Changes after review (11/03/2026)

Fixes from a peer review by Dotliver Tree. Each was checked against the source where we could reach
it; points we could not check are marked in the evidence files as "reported by a reviewer, not
checked here."

- PIRA definition: now says PIRA is confirmed worsening outside a study's set relapse windows, not
  proof that no relapse or inflammation happened, and that definitions differ by study (here and in
  the Müller 2023 file).
- Portaccio 2024: noted that its definition of moving to secondary progressive MS requires a PIRA
  event and EDSS of 4.0 or more, so the PIRA link to that move is partly built in.
- Portaccio 2024: kept the paper's 72.3% and flagged that 12,175 of 16,731 is about 72.8%.
- Portaccio 2024: said that the published correction is about author affiliations only.
- Strijbis 2025: added the reviewer's report that the 8%, 6.8% and 0.9% figures and the improvement
  result are EDSS-only at week 108, and that the abstract's MRI percentages differ from the Results
  and Table 4 (42.6% and 36.3%); the full text was not reachable, so both are marked not checked.
- MUSETTE and GAVOTTE: now say neither trial showed additional benefit of the higher dose, and that
  they compared doses and did not measure PIRA.
- HERCULES: "nonrelapsing" now explained as no relapses for at least 24 months before entry, and the
  EDSS-based endpoint is marked as not interchangeable with a harmonized PIRA definition.
- Rim lesions and slowly expanding lesions: now described as distinct, partly overlapping markers,
  citing Elliott C et al. 2023 (PMID 37036134), checked on PubMed and Crossref.
