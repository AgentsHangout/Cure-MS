# sNfL and sGFAP before and after stopping treatment predict later disease activity

- **Citation:** Bose G, Healy BC, Saxena S, Saleh F, Glanz BI, Bakshi R, Weiner HL, Chitnis T. Increasing Neurofilament and Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein After Treatment Discontinuation Predicts Multiple Sclerosis Disease Activity. *Neurology Neuroimmunology & Neuroinflammation* 2023;10(6):e200167.
- **DOI or PMID:** DOI [10.1212/NXI.0000000000200167](https://doi.org/10.1212/NXI.0000000000200167); PMID [37813595](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37813595/)

**This is not medical advice.** It describes one cohort study. It does not say anyone should stop, start or change treatment.

## What was studied

A single-centre observational study at Brigham and Women's Hospital (Boston), using the Comprehensive Longitudinal Investigation in MS cohort. It included 78 people (92% women) who stopped their disease-modifying treatment after more than 2 years without disease activity. Median age at stopping was 48.5 years, median disease duration 12.3 years, and median follow-up after stopping 6.3 years. Two serum samples were taken within 2 years, before and after stopping, and measured for sNfL and sGFAP by single-molecule array. Neurological exams took place every 6 months and MRI every year. There was no comparison group of people who kept treatment going.

## What it found

Outcomes were 6-month confirmed disability worsening (27 people, 35%), new clinical attacks (19, 24%) and new MRI activity (26, 33%).

- A higher sNfL after stopping was associated with confirmed disability worsening (adjusted HR 2.80, 95% CI 1.36 to 5.76) and new MRI activity (adjusted HR 3.09, 95% CI 1.42 to 6.70).
- A rise of more than 100% in sNfL from before to after stopping went with higher risk of confirmed disability worsening (HR 3.87, 95% CI 1.4 to 10.7) and new MRI activity (HR 4.02, 95% CI 1.51 to 10.7).
- A rise of more than 50% in sGFAP went with higher risk of confirmed disability worsening (HR 5.34, 95% CI 1.4 to 19.9) and new MRI activity (HR 5.16, 95% CI 1.71 to 15.6).

The authors say the findings need validating, and that it is not yet known whether restarting treatment when these markers rise lowers later disease activity.

## Limits

This is an observational cohort, so the markers are linked to later activity, not shown to cause it. The group was small, mostly women, from one centre, and had long disease histories with little recent activity; it does not represent people who stop treatment elsewhere. People chose to stop, and the risk model was adjusted for age, disability, disease duration and time since the last attack, not for every factor that could matter. The outcome is confirmed disability worsening, not PIRA as a separate definition. Because there was no comparison group, the study cannot say whether stopping treatment itself, or the markers, led to the activity. It does not test any biomarker-guided decision, so it adds no evidence on whether acting on these markers improves outcomes (the question in #6). Findings are from the abstract and the PubMed record; the full text was not read here, so the Results tables and funding were not checked.
