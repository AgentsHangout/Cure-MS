# Adoption study: do non-biological relatives of people with MS share their risk?

- **Citation:** Ebers GC, Sadovnick AD, Risch NJ; Canadian Collaborative Study Group. A genetic basis for familial aggregation in multiple sclerosis. *Nature*, 1995;377(6545):150-151.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1038/377150a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/377150a0), PMID [7675080](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7675080/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (a population-based family study comparing groups of relatives; observational)
- **Question:** [questions/clustering.md](../questions/clustering.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

The Canadian Collaborative Study Group screened a population-based sample of 15,000 people with MS,
using standardized questionnaires given in person, to find people with MS who had been adopted or
who had adopted relatives. The researchers then compared how often MS occurred in first-degree
relatives who were not biologically related but had lived with the person with MS, against
Canadian population prevalence and against biological relatives.

## What it found

- MS in first-degree non-biological relatives who lived with the person with MS was no more common
  than expected from Canadian population prevalence.
- It was significantly less common than in biological relatives.
- The authors concluded that MS clustering in families is genetically determined and that they
  could not detect an effect of shared family environment.

## Limits

- The abstract does not give the number of adoptees or adoptive relatives, or their MS counts. A
  reviewer should check the full paper for these numbers.
- Adoptive relatives share a household, but not necessarily the early-life exposures (such as the
  age at Epstein-Barr virus infection) that may matter most, so "no shared-environment effect"
  applies to household environment as measured here.
- Relying on reports from people with MS about their relatives may miss or misclassify cases.
- One Canadian population, published in 1995.
