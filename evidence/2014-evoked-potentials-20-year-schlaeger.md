# Evoked potentials and disability 20 years later

- **Citation:** Schlaeger R, Schindler C, Grize L, et al. Combined visual and motor evoked potentials predict multiple sclerosis disability after 20 years. *Multiple Sclerosis*, 2014;20(10):1348-1354.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1177/1352458514525867](https://doi.org/10.1177/1352458514525867), PMID [24574192](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24574192/)
- **Evidence level:** 3
- **Question:** [questions/repair.md](../questions/repair.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A cohort of 28 people with clinically definite MS in Basel. Visual and motor evoked potentials were
measured at entry and at 6, 12 and 24 months, and EDSS disability scores at entry, year 14 and year
20. The authors combined the evoked potential latencies at entry into one score and tested whether it
predicted EDSS at 20 years.

## What it found

- People with longer combined latencies at entry were more likely to have higher EDSS at 20 years
  (rho = 0.72, p < 0.0001) and a larger rise in EDSS (rho = 0.50, p = 0.006).
- In a model with age and later disease-modifying therapy, the entry latency score helped predict
  EDSS at 20 years (R squared 0.58; area under the curve 0.89). Entry EDSS and entry MRI lesion counts
  did not add to the prediction. Because the model includes treatment received during years 2 to
  20, it is not a prediction that could be made from entry data alone.

## Limits

- Only 28 people, from one center.
- It shows that slow signals at one time point, a sign of damage, go with later disability. It does
  not show that a *change* in latency, such as recovery after repair, goes with better function, so
  it does not close the repair gap.
- An association in a cohort, not cause. Treatment between years 2 and 20 was not randomized.
- The model was not tested in a separate group of people, so its accuracy may be overstated.
- The model uses treatment received after entry, so it cannot be used at entry to forecast one
  person's disability.
