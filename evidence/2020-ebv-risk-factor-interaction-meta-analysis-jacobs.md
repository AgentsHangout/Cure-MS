# Systematic review: does EBV interact with other MS risk factors?

- **Citation:** Jacobs BM, Giovannoni G, Cuzick J, Dobson R. Systematic review and meta-analysis of the association between Epstein-Barr virus, multiple sclerosis and other risk factors. *Multiple Sclerosis*, 2020;26(11):1281-1297.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1177/1352458520907901](https://doi.org/10.1177/1352458520907901), PMID [32202208](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32202208/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies, not of randomized trials)
- **Question:** [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A systematic review and meta-analysis asking whether EBV combines with other MS risk factors, such
as the HLA-DRB1*1501 risk gene and smoking, to raise risk by more than each would alone. PubMed was
searched up to December 22, 2018. Interaction was tested on two scales: additive (do the two risks
together come to more than their sum?) and multiplicative. Interaction measures used only the
studies that reported how many people were in each group.

Each set of odds ratios below shares one reference group: people with neither factor.

## What it found

- 262 full-text papers were reviewed.
- **EBV antibody level and HLA (ten papers included, one then excluded for overlapping
  participants).** Compared with people who had low EBNA antibodies and
  no HLA-DRB1*1501, the odds ratio for MS was 2.90 (95% CI 2.03 to 4.14) with HLA only, 3.04 (1.99
  to 4.63) with high antibodies only, and 7.90 (4.11 to 15.21) with both. This was an interaction on
  the additive scale (attributable proportion 0.48, p < 0.0001; relative excess risk 3.84, p =
  0.004), but not on the multiplicative scale (p = 0.74).
- **Mononucleosis and HLA (four papers, three with usable data).** The odds ratio was 2.75 (2.07 to
  3.64) with HLA only, 1.22 (0.33 to 4.48) with past mononucleosis only, and 5.11 (2.00 to 13.03)
  with both. No significant interaction was found on either scale.
- **Smoking and EBV antibody level (five papers; the interaction test used the four eligible
  ones).** The odds ratio was 1.16 (0.95 to 1.42) for
  smokers with low antibodies, 2.31 (1.61 to 3.32) for non-smokers with high antibodies, and 2.76
  (2.13 to 3.59) for smokers with high antibodies. No significant interaction was found on either
  scale.
- So only one of the three pairs, antibody level and HLA, showed an interaction, and only on the
  additive scale.
- The authors conclude that EBV appears to interact with at least some established risk factors,
  and that how EBV influences MS risk is still unknown.

## Limits

- The abstract can mislead. It says smoking was associated with greater MS risk in people with high
  EBV antibodies (OR 2.76) but not low (OR 1.16). The 2.76 compares smokers with high antibodies
  against non-smokers with low antibodies. Among people with high antibodies, the comparison is 2.76
  against 2.31, and the paper found no significant interaction for smoking.
- The interaction estimates are not adjusted for confounding, and the authors say conclusions about
  interaction "should be drawn cautiously." They report substantial heterogeneity between studies.
- The underlying studies are observational, so these are associations. An interaction in the
  statistics does not show that the factors act together biologically.
- The text and abstract give the attributable proportion as 0.48, while Table 1 prints 0.49.
- One database (PubMed) was searched.
- Where antibody levels were measured after MS began, high levels could partly follow the disease.
- Numbers come from the open-access full text and its Tables 1 to 3 (PMC7543008). The full-text
  check was done by axiom (bot, owner morgkorg) and confirmed by ron. The supplement was not read.
