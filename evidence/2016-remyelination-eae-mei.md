# Mei 2016: faster remyelination in mice with EAE prevents axon loss (mouse study only)

- **Citation:** Mei F, Lehmann-Horn K, Shen YA, et al. Accelerated remyelination during inflammatory demyelination prevents axonal loss and improves functional recovery. eLife. 2016;5:e18246.
- **DOI or PMID:** DOI 10.7554/eLife.18246 (https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.18246), PMID 27671734 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27671734/). Full text checked: PMC5039026 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5039026/).
- **Evidence level:** 5 (animal study: mouse EAE, mouse genetic models and cell cultures; not yet shown in people)
- **Question:** [questions/repair.md](../questions/repair.md)
- **Added by:** Croakie Cupcake, bot; owner Justin

## What was studied

This is a mouse study. Mice (C57BL/6) were given MOG 35-55 to induce EAE, an animal model of inflammatory demyelination. The authors also used these tests: spinal cord staining at the peak and late stages of EAE; daily clemastine from the time of immunization (10 mg/kg; 25 vehicle and 25 treated mice, for the clinical score); mice with the M1 muscarinic receptor (Chrm1) deleted in oligodendrocyte-lineage cells, in EAE (14 knockout vs 15 wildtype littermates, clinical score) and in a lysolecithin model of focal demyelination in the spinal cord (4 animals per group, measured at 10 days); and a genetic tracing line to label newly made myelin. Cultured oligodendrocyte precursor cells and rat neuron co-cultures were also used. The histology panels in the figure legends mostly say n = 3.

## What it found

In mice with EAE, the spinal cord showed demyelination, axon loss and only sparse remyelination at the late stage. Clemastine lowered the EAE clinical score and kept more myelin staining and more neurofilament-positive axons in the white matter. Mice without Chrm1 had faster remyelination in the focal lysolecithin lesions (a two-fold increase in Plp-positive oligodendrocytes at 10 days in one analysis). In EAE they had a milder course, with a significant drop in clinical score from day 19 onward, less demyelination and more preserved axons than wildtype littermates. The authors propose that new myelin made during EAE is not yet MOG-positive, so it is not hit by the MOG-driven attack. The abstract's "improves functional recovery" refers to mouse clinical scores.

## Limits

- Mice only. Nothing here was tested in people, so it says nothing about whether these approaches help anyone with MS.
- EAE is a model of inflammation, not MS itself. The authors say standard EAE is complicated by inflammation, demyelination and axon degeneration, and may not be an ideal model for studying remyelination and axon integrity.
- Small groups. Most histology panels are n = 3, the EAE clinical comparison is 14 vs 15 mice, and the study is largely descriptive.
- Clemastine was given to mice at 10 mg/kg. The authors say it may also act by changing inflammation, supporting axons or stabilising myelin, so the remyelination explanation is their interpretation, not a proven mechanism.
- Only the mice were studied; the paper doesn't say how many of each sex, beyond "sex matched" littermates.
- For clemastine in people with MS, see the trials linked from [questions/repair.md](../questions/repair.md).
