# Stem cell transplant vs alemtuzumab in Sweden

- **Citation:** Zhukovsky C, Sandgren S, Silfverberg T, et al. Autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation compared with alemtuzumab for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis: an observational study. *Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery and Psychiatry*, 2021;92(2):189-194.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1136/jnnp-2020-323992](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2020-323992), PMID [33106366](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33106366/)
- **Evidence level:** 3
- **Question:** [questions/cell-therapy.md](../questions/cell-therapy.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

An observational study of people with relapsing-remitting MS treated with either autologous
hematopoietic stem cell transplant (aHSCT, 69 people, with cyclophosphamide and antithymocyte
globulin) or alemtuzumab (75 people, given as 2 or more yearly courses). People had at least yearly
checks of disability (EDSS), side effects and MRI. The main outcome was "no evidence of disease
activity" (NEDA: no relapses, no new MRI lesions and no disability worsening).

## What it found

- At 3 years, an estimated 88% of people treated with aHSCT and 37% treated with alemtuzumab still
  had no evidence of disease activity.
- Yearly relapse rate was 0.04 after aHSCT and 0.1 after alemtuzumab.
- At last follow-up, disability had improved / stayed stable / worsened in 57% / 41% / 1% after aHSCT
  and 45% / 43% / 12% after alemtuzumab. This difference was not statistically significant
  (P = 0.06).
- In the first 100 days, side effects of grade 3 or higher occurred in 48 of 69 people after aHSCT
  and in none of 75 after alemtuzumab.
- After that, the most common long-term side effect was thyroid disease, estimated at 21% after
  aHSCT and 46% after alemtuzumab at 3 years.
- People treated with aHSCT were more likely to stay free of disease activity. This is an
  association, not proof that aHSCT caused the difference.

## Limits

- Observational, not randomized, and not matched. According to the full text
  ([PMC7841472](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7841472/)), treatment mostly followed each
  center's local practice: Uppsala mainly used aHSCT and Sahlgrenska mainly used alemtuzumab, with
  exceptions (for example, alemtuzumab at Uppsala when aHSCT was judged unsuitable or the person
  asked for it). The groups differed at baseline: people given aHSCT were younger, had more relapses
  in the prior year and higher disability scores. The authors argue that allocation by center limits
  selection bias, but differences between centers and between the people treated may still explain
  part of the result.
- Small (144 people) and from Swedish centers only.
- Compares aHSCT with one drug only. It does not show how aHSCT compares with anti-CD20 antibodies
  such as ocrelizumab.
- Funded by Swedish medical societies, patient organizations, foundations and Swedish government
  research funds (ALF), according to the full text. Several authors report lecture or advisory fees
  from drug companies.
