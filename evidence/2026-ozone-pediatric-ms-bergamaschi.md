# Ozone exposure before pediatric-onset MS (PEDIGREE study)

- **Citation:** Bergamaschi R, Ferraro OE, Pisoni E, et al. (PEDIGREE Study Group). Ozone pollution as a possible trigger for multiple sclerosis in young people: the PEDIGREE study. J Neurol. 2026;273(3):172. Erratum: J Neurol. 2026;273(6):319.
- **DOI or PMID:** DOI 10.1007/s00415-026-13688-2 (https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-026-13688-2); PMID 41770337 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41770337/). Erratum: DOI 10.1007/s00415-026-13770-9, PMID 42149220.
- **Evidence level:** 3 (case-control study; association, not cause)
- **Question:** none linked yet

## What was studied

The PEDIGREE study, a multicentre Italian case-control study of MS diagnosed before age 18. Data were available for 113 pediatric-onset MS cases and 117 controls. Air pollution in the first, second and third years before MS onset (PM2.5, PM10, O3, NO2, SO2 and CO) was taken from the European Monitoring and Evaluation Programme (EMEP) database. Environmental and perinatal exposures came from the PEQ-IT questionnaire.

## What it found

Higher ozone (O3) exposure was associated with pediatric-onset MS in each of the three years before onset: year 1 OR 1.11 (95% CI 1.03-1.19; p = 0.007), year 2 OR 1.10 (1.02-1.18; p = 0.012), year 3 OR 1.09 (1.01-1.17; p = 0.025). The abstract says these held after adjusting for gender, age at onset, sun exposure, parental smoking and socioeconomic background. The authors describe this as roughly a 10% higher risk per one-unit increase in O3. This is an association, not proof that ozone causes MS.

## Limits

- Small sample: 113 cases and 117 controls, so the estimates are imprecise and sensitive to chance.
- Exposure is regional monitoring data from EMEP, not personal exposure. People spend time indoors and in different places, so this may misclassify what each child breathed.
- The abstract reports significant results for O3, and doesn't report the other pollutants. Testing several pollutants raises the chance of a false positive from multiple testing, and the abstract does not say whether other results were adjusted for this.
- Case-control design: exposure was looked at after onset, and recall and selection can bias the result. It shows an association only.
- Erratum: the journal published a correction (J Neurol 2026;273(6):319, DOI 10.1007/s00415-026-13770-9). I could only see the bibliographic record; the correction text was not available to me, so I have not checked what it changes.
- Checked against the PubMed abstract and Crossref metadata only. I did not read the full text, so the tables, the other pollutant results and the methods details are not verified here.
