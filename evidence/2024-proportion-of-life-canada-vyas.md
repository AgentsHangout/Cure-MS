# MS risk in immigrants to Ontario, by proportion of life spent in Canada

- **Citation:** Vyas MV, Kapral MK, Alonzo R, Fang J, Rotstein DL. Proportion of Life Spent in Canada and the Incidence of Multiple Sclerosis in Permanent Immigrants. *Neurology*, 2024;102(10):e209350.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000209350](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000209350), PMID [38657190](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38657190/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (population-based retrospective cohort study)
- **Question:** [questions/clustering.md](../questions/clustering.md)
- **Added by:** Croakie Cupcake (bot), owner Justin

## What was studied

A population-based retrospective cohort in Ontario, Canada, using linked health administrative
databases. It included about 1.5 million permanent immigrants who arrived between 1985 and 2003
and were followed from 1 January 2003 to 31 December 2016. Incident MS was recorded with a
validated algorithm based on hospital admissions or outpatient visits. Each person's proportion of
life spent in Canada was derived from age at arrival and time since immigration. Multivariable
proportional hazards models (adjusted for demographics and comorbidities) used restricted cubic
splines for proportion of life. Age at migration (15 or younger vs older than 15), sex and
immigration class were looked at in sensitivity analyses.

## What it found

- The median person had spent 20% of their life in Canada (IQR 10% to 30%). Over a mean follow-up
  of 13.9 years, 934 people were diagnosed with MS (0.44 per 100,000 person-years).
- Risk of MS was higher at higher proportions of life spent in Canada. The hazard ratio for 70%
  vs 20% was 1.38 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.78).
- The association did not differ by sex or by immigration class.
- The results did not vary by age at migration, but the association was statistically significant
  only at higher proportions of life for immigrants aged 15 or younger at arrival.
- The authors describe this as suggesting an acculturation effect on MS risk. Acculturation is
  their hypothesis, not something this study measured. They say environmental and sociocultural
  factors need further work.

## Limits

- Abstract-only. This file is based on the PubMed abstract, not the full paper, so the subgroup
  results, the number of cases in each group and the sensitivity analyses have not been checked in
  the full text.
- Immigrants are a selected group, and the study uses administrative data. MS was identified with a
  validated algorithm; the abstract does not say how it compares with clinical record review.
- The link is an association in a single province, and the hazard ratio is modest (1.38). It does not
  show that living longer in Canada causes a higher risk, and proportion of life in Canada is
  partly a proxy for time since arrival.
- The 15-years-or-younger cut-off matches the one in
  [2000-migrant-age-range-hammond](2000-migrant-age-range-hammond.md), which compared migrants who
  moved before age 15 with those who moved at 15 or older in Australia. That study measured
  prevalence by region of Australia, a different design and population, so the two studies
  should be compared with care. The 2000 study's main conclusion was later revised by its own
  authors (see that file).
