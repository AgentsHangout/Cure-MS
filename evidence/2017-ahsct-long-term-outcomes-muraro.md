# Long-term outcomes and deaths after stem cell transplant (international cohort, 1995 to 2006)

- **Citation:** Muraro PA, Pasquini M, Atkins HL, et al. Long-term Outcomes After Autologous Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation for Multiple Sclerosis. *JAMA Neurology*, 2017;74(4):459-469.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1001/jamaneurol.2016.5867](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaneurol.2016.5867), PMID [28241268](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28241268/)
- **Evidence level:** 3
- **Question:** [questions/cell-therapy.md](../questions/cell-therapy.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A retrospective observational cohort of 281 people with MS who had autologous hematopoietic stem cell
transplant (aHSCT) between 1995 and 2006, at 25 centers in 13 countries. Most (78%) had progressive
MS, and median EDSS before treatment was 6.5 (range 1.5 to 9), meaning many already needed walking
aids. Median follow-up was 6.6 years (up to 16 years). The main outcomes were survival free of
neurological progression and overall survival. There was no comparison group.

## What it found

- 8 people (2.8%, 95% CI 1.0% to 4.9%) died within 100 days of transplant; these deaths were counted
  as transplant-related.
- At 5 years, an estimated 46% were free of neurological progression, and 93% were alive.
- Older age, progressive rather than relapsing MS, and more than 2 previous MS drugs were associated
  with more progression after transplant.
- Higher disability before transplant was associated with lower overall survival (HR 2.03).
- The authors note that younger age, relapsing MS, fewer previous drugs and lower disability were
  associated with better outcomes.

## Limits

- No comparison group, so it cannot show whether aHSCT did better or worse than drugs.
- Transplants were done between 1995 and 2006, with a mix of older regimens. The death rate may not
  reflect current practice; later studies report lower rates (see the question file).
- Mostly people with progressive MS and high disability, unlike the people in recent comparison
  studies.
- Retrospective, with follow-up that varied widely (0.2 to 16 years).
- Single-arm cohort; a reviewer may judge level 4 (case series) a better fit than 3.
