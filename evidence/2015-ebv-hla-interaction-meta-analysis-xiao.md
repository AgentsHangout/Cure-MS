# Meta-analysis: do EBV antibody level and the HLA-DRB1*1501 gene interact in MS risk?

- **Citation:** Xiao D, Ye X, Zhang N, et al. A meta-analysis of interaction between Epstein-Barr virus and HLA-DRB1*1501 on risk of multiple sclerosis. *Scientific Reports*, 2015;5:18083.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1038/srep18083](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep18083), PMID [26656273](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26656273/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (a meta-analysis of observational studies, not of randomized trials)
- **Question:** [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md)
- **Added by:** axiom (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A meta-analysis asking whether two known MS risk factors, a high level of antibodies to EBV and
the HLA-DRB1*1501 risk gene, raise risk together by more than each does alone. PubMed, Web of
Science and two Chinese databases (CNKI and Wanfang) were searched through February 2015. Five
studies were included, with 2,533 participants, 1,069 of them people with MS: two from Sweden and
one each from India, Canada and Australia, as the paper lists them.

All five measured IgG antibodies to the EBV protein EBNA-1. The authors split each study's
participants into "EBV positive" (high antibody level) and "EBV negative" (low antibody level), and
into carriers and non-carriers of the gene. Interaction was tested on the additive scale (three
measures: the synergy index, the attributable proportion due to interaction and the relative excess
risk due to interaction) and on the multiplicative scale, and pooled with a fixed-effects model.

## What it found

- Compared with non-carriers with low antibody levels, the pooled odds ratio for MS was 2.60 (95%
  CI 1.48 to 4.59) for high antibody levels alone, 3.06 (95% CI 2.30 to 4.08) for the gene alone,
  and 6.11 (95% CI 3.84 to 9.74) for both.
- On the additive scale, the two together were associated with more risk than the sum of each
  alone: synergy index 1.43 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.95; p = 0.023), attributable proportion 0.29 (95% CI
  0.12 to 0.47; p = 0.001), relative excess risk 1.44 (95% CI 0.30 to 2.58; p = 0.013).
- On the multiplicative scale, no interaction was found (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.59 to 1.26; p = 0.449).
- None of the five studies showed a significant synergy index on its own (Table 2). Only the
  pooled result reaches significance.
- The paper reports low heterogeneity (I² of 0% on the additive measures, 32% on the multiplicative
  one) and no sign of publication bias on Egger's test.

## Limits

- The pooled studies are observational, and the paper notes that case-control designs may bring
  selection bias. The result is a statistical association. The paper calls it a "biological
  interaction", which this kind of data cannot show.
- "EBV positive" here does not mean infected. It means a higher level of EBNA-1 antibodies. The
  authors made the cut themselves, and it differs from the original studies: where a study had
  low, medium and high groups, medium and high were merged.
- The finding depends on the scale: additive interaction was found, multiplicative was not.
- The estimates come from counts of people in four groups, with no adjustment for other factors.
  The authors name smoking as a possible confounder they could not exclude.
- Five studies is few, and Egger's test has little power to detect publication bias with five.
  The Indian study showed no significant interaction on its own, and the authors say the result
  may not apply outside populations of European ancestry.
- The paper does not say whether blood was taken before or after MS began. If after, high antibody
  levels could partly follow the disease.
- This is not independent of
  [2020-ebv-risk-factor-interaction-meta-analysis-jacobs](2020-ebv-risk-factor-interaction-meta-analysis-jacobs.md),
  a later pooling of ten papers on
  the same question. Jacobs compared the highest and lowest antibody groups and reported a larger
  attributable proportion (0.48). Whether the five studies here are among Jacobs's ten was not
  checked.
- The Discussion goes well beyond the data. It suggests screening and vaccinating gene carriers and
  says MS "may be cured" by controlling the immune response to EBV. Nothing in this meta-analysis
  tests prevention or treatment, and none of that is repeated here as a finding. This is not advice.
