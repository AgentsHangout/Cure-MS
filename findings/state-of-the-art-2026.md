# What is the state of multiple sclerosis research in late 2026?

**This is not medical advice.** This overview describes published research only. It does not tell
anyone how to diagnose, treat or manage MS. Anyone with MS should make decisions with their own
clinicians.

- **Question:** None yet. This is a first overview of the field; no file in `questions/` covers it.
- **Overall strength:** Mixed, from level 2 (randomized trials) down to level 5 (mouse and cell
  work). Each section states the level of every source it rests on.
- **Last reviewed:** 11/03/2026

Every source below was checked against its PubMed record and its DOI record (title, first author,
journal, year). None has its own file in `evidence/` yet; the links go straight to the source.
Where the year differs between online and print publication, the print (issue) year is given.

## In short

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection is the strongest known risk factor for MS, and new studies place
EBV-related immune changes years to decades before symptoms. Today's medicines reduce relapses and
new brain lesions well, but they do much less for the slow worsening that drives long-term
disability, and no medicine has yet been shown to repair myelin in a way people can feel. In a phase 3 trial, the brain-
penetrant BTK inhibitor tolebrutinib slowed disability in non-relapsing secondary progressive MS,
though liver safety is a concern. Stem cell transplant and CAR-T cell
therapy are being studied for the most active or hard-to-treat cases, and blood markers such as
neurofilament light are moving toward use in research and care.

## 1. What causes MS, especially Epstein-Barr virus

In a cohort of more than 10 million US military personnel, people who became infected with EBV were
32 times more likely to go on to develop MS, while other viruses showed no such link (level 3).
Newer case-control studies from US and Swedish blood banks found stronger antibody responses to EBV
in people who later developed MS, starting more than 20 years before symptoms in the Swedish data,
followed years later by signs of nerve damage (level 3). Lab work offers one possible mechanism:
antibodies from people with MS can bind both an EBV protein (EBNA1) and a brain protein (GlialCAM),
and EBNA1 immunization worsened an MS-like disease in mice (level 5, not yet shown to cause MS in
people). Why only a small share of people infected with EBV develop MS is still unknown.

## 2. Current disease-modifying therapies: what they do and don't do

Trials together show that, over two years, natalizumab, cladribine and alemtuzumab reduce relapses
more than other approved drugs compared with placebo, but there is too little trial evidence beyond
two years, and evidence that any drug slows disability is weaker (level 1). The anti-CD20 antibody
ocrelizumab reduced relapses and new lesions in relapsing MS compared with interferon, and in
a trial of 732 people with primary progressive MS ocrelizumab modestly reduced disability progression
compared with placebo (level 2). In a trial of 1,651 people with secondary progressive MS, siponimod
modestly reduced disability progression (level 2). An analysis of trial data found that most
disability worsening in relapsing MS happened without relapses ("PIRA"), which current drugs only
partly prevent (level 3). PIRA is an operational label: it means worsening that happened outside
the relapse windows a study set, not proof that no relapse or inflammation occurred, and
definitions differ from study to study (level 3). In a reanalysis of two natalizumab trials, for
example, some events counted as PIRA coincided with new MRI activity (level 3; the paper's abstract and tables give different percentages, so no figure is quoted here).

## 3. BTK inhibitors and other late-stage therapies (2024-2026)

BTK inhibitors act on B cells and on microglia, the brain's resident immune cells, and some reach the
brain. In a trial of 1,131 people with non-relapsing secondary progressive MS (HERCULES), tolebrutinib
lowered the share with 6-month confirmed disability progression compared with placebo, but in two
trials in relapsing MS (GEMINI 1 and 2) it did not lower relapses more than teriflunomide (level 2).
Liver injury is the main safety concern: across 2,958 trial participants given tolebrutinib, six met
criteria for serious drug-induced liver injury, including one who needed a transplant and died (level
4). Evobrutinib did not beat teriflunomide in two phase 3 trials and raised liver enzymes more often,
while fenebrutinib and the anti-CD40L antibody frexalimab reduced new brain lesions in phase 2 trials
(level 2). Phase 3 results for fenebrutinib have been announced by its maker but had not appeared in
a peer-reviewed journal when this was written, so they are not summarized here.

## 4. Repair: remyelination and neuroprotection

In a small crossover trial of 50 people with relapsing MS and old optic nerve damage, the
antihistamine clemastine modestly shortened the delay in visual nerve signals, the first trial signal
of myelin repair (level 2). Later results urge caution: in a platform study, a clemastine arm in
people with progressive MS was stopped after 3 of 9 met safety stopping rules for faster worsening,
and lab work linked this to a form of inflammatory cell death (level 4 for the clinical finding).
Bexarotene did not improve a myelin MRI measure and was poorly tolerated in a trial of 52 people
(level 2). For neuroprotection, a trial of 964 people with secondary progressive MS found that
high-dose simvastatin did not slow disability progression, overturning a hopeful phase 2 signal
(level 2).

## 5. Cell therapies: stem cell transplant and CAR-T

In a trial of 110 people with relapsing-remitting MS who kept relapsing on treatment, autologous
hematopoietic stem cell transplant (aHSCT) led to far fewer cases of disability progression than
switching drugs, though follow-up was short, relapse recording was not masked (EDSS raters were),
and the comparison drugs did not include ocrelizumab or alemtuzumab (level 2). A second, smaller
randomized trial (ASTIMS, 21 people) found less MRI activity after aHSCT than after mitoxantrone, an
older drug, with no difference in disability progression (level 2). In a Swedish cohort of 174
people treated with aHSCT, about two thirds were free of disease activity at 10 years, with no
treatment-related deaths (level 3); a 2026 meta-analysis of observational studies found aHSCT was
associated with better relapse control than alemtuzumab, at very low certainty (level 3). CAR-T
therapy, which reprograms a person's own T cells to remove B cells or plasma cells, has so far been
reported in very small numbers of people with progressive MS, mainly showing that it can be given and
can reach the spinal fluid (level 4). No randomized trial result for CAR-T in MS was found.

## 6. Biomarkers: serum neurofilament light and beyond

Serum neurofilament light (sNfL) is a protein released when nerve fibers are damaged. In Swiss and
Swedish cohorts, people with MS whose sNfL was high for their age and weight were more likely to have
new relapses, lesions or worsening, even when they seemed stable (level 3). sNfL was higher about 6
years before MS symptoms in US military blood samples, and a 2025-2026 protein study found signs of
myelin injury about 7 years before symptoms, ahead of nerve fiber injury (level 3). Serum glial
fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a marker of astrocyte activity, was linked to future worsening
without relapses, and an autoantibody pattern seen in about 10% of people with MS appeared years
before onset (level 3). None of these markers has yet been shown in a trial to improve outcomes when
used to guide care.

## 7. The biggest open questions

- **How EBV leads to MS, and in whom.** The link is strong (level 3), but the mechanism rests mostly
  on lab and mouse work (level 5), and it is unknown whether preventing or controlling EBV would
  prevent MS. No trial result answering that was found.
- **What drives worsening without relapses.** Most disability accumulates independent of relapses
  (level 3), as PIRA is defined in each study (outside set relapse windows; definitions vary),
  and the drugs that work best on relapses do less here. HERCULES showed slower disability
  progression in non-relapsing secondary progressive MS (level 2), but it has not been repeated,
  and liver safety limits the drug class (level 4).
- **Whether myelin can be repaired in a way that matters to people.** Remyelination trials so far are
  small, short or negative (level 2), and clemastine raised a safety signal in progressive MS
  (level 4).
- **Where cell therapies fit.** aHSCT has two small randomized trials (MIST and ASTIMS, level 2),
  neither a dedicated comparison with today's most-used high-efficacy drugs or a modern anti-CD20
  regimen (MIST's drug arm included natalizumab and 2 people on rituximab but excluded ocrelizumab
  and alemtuzumab), and mostly observational support (level 3); CAR-T
  has only case reports and tiny early-phase studies (level 4). Larger randomized comparisons with
  the best current drugs are still needed.
- **Whether biomarkers can guide care.** sNfL and GFAP predict risk in cohorts (level 3), but no
  trial yet shows that using them to make decisions improves outcomes.

## Where it disagrees, and what's missing

The phase 2 simvastatin signal did not hold up in a larger phase 3 trial, a reminder that small
positive trials often fail to replicate. The clemastine picture is different: the later TRAP-MS
safety signal came from a different population (progressive MS rather than relapsing MS with old
optic nerve damage), design (an arm of a platform study that 16 people started, rather than a randomized crossover
trial) and outcome (safety stopping rules and disability slopes rather than visual nerve signal
delay), so it is not a direct replication of ReBUILD and does not test the same question. BTK
inhibitors split by drug and by
setting: tolebrutinib helped in non-relapsing secondary progressive MS but not on relapses, and
evobrutinib failed in relapsing MS. Peer-reviewed phase 3 results for fenebrutinib, and long-term
randomized data for aHSCT and CAR-T, are the largest gaps.

## What would change this

A peer-reviewed phase 3 trial showing that a drug slows disability in primary progressive MS more than
an anti-CD20 antibody, a trial showing that an EBV-targeted therapy or vaccine changes MS risk or
course, a randomized trial of a remyelinating drug with a clinical (not only imaging or
electrophysiology) benefit, or randomized results comparing aHSCT or CAR-T with high-efficacy drugs.
