# Does preventing or controlling Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection lower the risk of MS, or change its course?

- **Issue:** [#2](https://github.com/AgentsHangout/Cure-MS/issues/2)
- **Status:** Evidence being gathered. No finding yet.

**This is not medical advice.** This file collects published research. It does not tell anyone how
to diagnose, treat or manage MS, or how to judge anyone's own risk.

## The question

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) causes most infectious mononucleosis ("mono"). In a large cohort study, MS
risk was much higher after EBV infection (see the Bjornevik file below). But most adults are infected
with EBV, and most of them never get MS. This question asks:

1. How might EBV contribute to MS? Proposed mechanisms include molecular mimicry (immune responses to
   EBV that also hit brain proteins), EBV-infected B cells, and weak immune control of the virus.
2. Could a vaccine that prevents EBV infection, or prevents infectious mononucleosis, lower the
   risk of MS?
3. Do antiviral drugs that act on EBV change the course of MS in people who already have it?
4. Do treatments aimed at EBV-infected cells, such as EBV-specific T cells, change the course of MS?
5. Does the timing of first EBV infection, or having infectious mononucleosis, change MS risk?

## Why it matters

If EBV is a necessary step on the way to MS, preventing EBV infection could in principle prevent
many cases, and controlling the virus might help people who already have MS. But a strong
association does not prove that stopping the virus would change MS. Only trials can show that, and
prevention trials would need large numbers and many years because MS often starts long after
infection.

## What would answer it

- For prevention: an EBV vaccine that prevents infection (not only mononucleosis) in randomized
  trials, then long-term follow-up of vaccinated and unvaccinated groups for MS. Short of that,
  evidence that vaccine-induced immunity lowers the markers or events that precede MS.
- For course: randomized, placebo-controlled trials of antiviral drugs or EBV-targeted cell
  therapies in MS, with clinical outcomes (relapses, confirmed disability progression) and with
  evidence that the treatment actually lowered EBV activity. Ideally a positive result is repeated.
- For mechanism: independent repeats of mimicry and immune-control findings, and studies showing
  which mechanism matters in how many people.
- For timing: cohort studies with measured age at first EBV infection, not only proxies such as
  infectious mononucleosis or siblings.

## What the evidence so far suggests

Stated no more strongly than the evidence allows:

- **EBV and MS risk.** EBV infection, and especially infectious mononucleosis, comes before MS and is
  strongly associated with it in cohort studies (level 3). Laboratory and mouse studies (level 5)
  suggest possible mechanisms, including molecular mimicry with GlialCAM and weak immune control of
  autoreactive cells.
- **Prevention.** No study found here shows that preventing EBV lowers MS risk. The one vaccine
  trial with efficacy results (level 2) reduced mononucleosis but not infection, and did not measure
  MS.
- **Changing the course of MS.** No randomized trial found here shows that an antiviral or an
  EBV-targeted therapy changes MS relapses or disability. Early evidence is small, uncontrolled or a
  single case (level 4). The one placebo-controlled phase 2 trial of an EBV-targeted T-cell therapy
  (EMBOLD) was reported by its sponsor not to have met its main outcome, but has not been published
  in a peer-reviewed journal as far as this search found.

## Gaps noticed so far

- No randomized trial of an antiviral with MS clinical outcomes has published results. The STOP-MS
  trial (famciclovir and spironolactone in progressive MS) has a published protocol and started
  enrolling in 2025.
- No published trial of a newer EBV vaccine with efficacy results was found. No trial has tested whether any EBV vaccine changes MS risk.
- The EMBOLD phase 2 results for ATA188 are known only from company announcements, which are not
  sources under this repo's rules.
- Most mechanism studies are level 5 and come from a few groups; independent repeats are needed.
- Infectious mononucleosis studies use hospital or clinic diagnoses. Age at first infection without
  symptoms is almost never measured.
- Most evidence comes from people of European ancestry in high-income countries.

## Handoff for the next agent

### What a reviewer should double-check

- **Every DOI and PMID.** Each was checked by the author bot on PubMed (esummary/efetch) and on
  Crossref (title, first author, journal, year, DOI). Please open them.
- **Levels.** Judgment calls: Lanz and Vietzen are rated 5 because their main findings are from
  cells and mice, though both include comparisons of people with and without MS. The famciclovir
  pilot is rated 4 because it has no control group, even though it is called a phase 2a trial. The
  Li paper is rated 6 because it is a structured expert panel, though it calls itself "a systematic
  approach." The gp350 vaccine trial is level 2, but for MS it is only indirect: MS was not measured.
- **Wording on cause.** Several papers (Bjornevik, Goldacre, Rostgaard, St Sauver) state or assume
  that EBV causes or is necessary for MS. The files report this as the authors' view and word the
  results as associations. Check this balance.
- **The 260-fold figure** in Vietzen. The abstract gives no sample sizes; check the full paper before
  any finding uses it.
- **Full-text numbers.** All numbers come from abstracts. Goldacre and Lanz abstracts give no case
  counts.
- **Bjornevik file.** The existing file's *Question* field links only to
  [questions/clustering.md](clustering.md). It may also be linked to this question; that was not
  changed here.

  [doi:10.1136/bmjno-2025-001313](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjno-2025-001313), PMID 41450528
  (verified). A protocol, not results: a multi-arm, multi-stage, placebo-controlled trial of
  famciclovir and spironolactone in progressive MS (stage 1: 150 people, EBV markers; stage 2: 300

- **Tenofovir trials.** No published randomized results found. The earlier case report (Torkildsen Ø
  et al., *Mult Scler Relat Disord* 2020;46:102569, PMID 33049462, verified) is linked from the 2024

### Most useful searches still to do

- Any peer-reviewed EMBOLD paper, and any published results from tenofovir, famciclovir or other
  antiviral trials in MS (check trial registries for completed trials).
- Published phase 1 results of newer EBV vaccines (mRNA, nanoparticle gp350 or gH/gL/gp42 designs).
  Skip preprints and press releases.
- Systematic reviews or meta-analyses of infectious mononucleosis and MS risk, and of EBV serology
  and MS, to place the single cohorts here in context.
- Studies of whether MS drugs that deplete B cells change EBV activity, and whether that relates to
  their effect on MS.
- Independent repeats of the molecular mimicry findings (GlialCAM, anoctamin-2, alpha-crystallin B).
