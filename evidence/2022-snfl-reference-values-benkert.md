# Reference values for serum neurofilament light, and what high values predict

- **Citation:** Benkert P, Meier S, Schaedelin S, et al. Serum neurofilament light chain for individual prognostication of disease activity in people with multiple sclerosis: a retrospective modelling and validation study. *The Lancet Neurology*, 2022;21(3):246-257.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1016/S1474-4422(22)00009-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(22)00009-6), PMID [35182510](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35182510/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (a modeling study built on cohorts, with retrospective analysis of cohort data and validation in a registry)
- **Question:** [questions/biomarkers.md](../questions/biomarkers.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

Serum neurofilament light (sNfL) rises with age, so one raw value means different things at 25 and
at 65. The authors built a reference database from people with no sign of central nervous system
disease in four cohort studies in Europe and North America (10,133 samples from 5,390 people). They
modeled how sNfL changes with age and body-mass index (BMI) to turn any value into a percentile or a
Z score (how far it is from the expected value for someone of that age and BMI).

They then applied these scores to 7,769 samples from 1,313 people with MS in the Swiss Multiple
Sclerosis Cohort and checked whether higher scores went with later relapses, new MRI lesions or
disability worsening. They repeated the analysis in 4,341 people in the Swedish MS registry.

## What it found

- In the control group, sNfL rose exponentially with age, and more steeply after about age 50.
- In people with MS, higher percentiles and Z scores went with a gradually higher risk of later
  relapses and new lesions and of later disability worsening.
- An sNfL Z score above 1.5 was associated with a higher risk of later clinical or MRI activity
  (odds ratio 3.15, 95% CI 2.35-4.23). This held in people who looked stable, with no evidence of
  disease activity (odds ratio 2.66, 95% CI 1.08-6.55).
- Z scores did better than fixed raw cutoffs.
- At the group level, Z scores fell to levels seen in controls in people on monoclonal antibodies
  (alemtuzumab, natalizumab, ocrelizumab, rituximab), fell less on oral drugs (dimethyl fumarate,
  fingolimod, siponimod, teriflunomide), and stayed raised on interferons and glatiramer acetate.
- The Swedish registry results supported the Swiss findings.

## Limits

- Observational and retrospective. A high Z score is linked to higher risk; this study does not test
  whether changing treatment because of a high score improves outcomes.
- The drug comparisons are not randomized, so differences between people who got each drug may
  explain part of the gaps between drug groups.
- The reference model adjusts for age and BMI only. Other factors that affect sNfL, such as kidney
  function, are not mentioned in the abstract.
- Funding included the Swiss National Science Foundation and the Progressive MS Alliance, and also
  Biogen, Celgene, Novartis and Roche, which make MS drugs.
- All numbers come from the abstract.
