**This is not medical advice.** Everything below is an untested research hypothesis: an idea for
what future studies could test, not a finding that any treatment or combination works. Nothing here

# Which ideas for stopping or reversing MS are best supported, and what would test them next?

- **Question:** None yet; this synthesis draws on [questions/pira.md](../questions/pira.md),
  [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md), [questions/repair.md](../questions/repair.md),
  [questions/cell-therapy.md](../questions/cell-therapy.md) and
  [questions/biomarkers.md](../questions/biomarkers.md).
- **Overall strength:** Level 6 (hypotheses). Individual steps rest on evidence from level 2 down to
  level 5, and every hypothesis except two (1 and 5) has at least one step with no supporting
  evidence at all. No hypothesis here has been tested from start to finish.
- **Last reviewed:** 11/03/2026

## In short

Seven hypotheses are ranked below. The best supported is that a one-time immune "reset" with
autologous stem cell transplant (aHSCT) can halt inflammatory activity for years in highly active
relapsing MS, though it has not been shown to beat today's best drugs. Next is a two-compartment
combination: B-cell depletion outside the brain plus a brain-penetrant BTK inhibitor aimed at
smoldering lesions, to slow worsening without relapses. Its parts each have trial support, but the
combination itself has never been tested. Ideas built on myelin repair, nerve protection, CAR-T and
Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) control rest on weaker or missing links: the CAR-T chain now has a reported
clinical measure at every step, but only uncontrolled measures in 5 people, which cannot show
benefit. **The ranking is a judgment made
with a stated rubric. It is not a result, and a single new trial could reorder it.**

## How the hypotheses are scored

Each hypothesis is broken into a chain of steps. Each step is given the **strongest** evidence level
among the files that support it ([docs/evidence-levels.md](../docs/evidence-levels.md)); a step with
no supporting file is marked "none." Evidence that points against a step is listed but does not count
as support. Four criteria, each scored 0 to 3 (maximum 12):

| Criterion | 3 | 2 | 1 | 0 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Weakest link** (the lowest-level step) | Level 1 or 2 | Level 3 | Level 4 | Level 5, 6 or no evidence |
| **B. Steps resting on level 1 or 2** | Two thirds or more of steps | A third to under two thirds | At least one, under a third | None |
| **C. Safety known** | Randomized safety data in hundreds of similar people, no unresolved serious signal | Quantified in trials or large cohorts, but with a serious known risk, or known only for the parts of a combination | Only small series or small trials | No data, or an unresolved harm signal in the people who would be studied |
| **D. Can be tested soon** | Existing agents; a randomized trial with a clinical outcome could report within about 5 years | Needs one earlier-phase step, or 5 to 10 years | Needs several steps, or over 10 years | No suitable agent, or not feasible |

Scores are coarse and ties are possible. Different reasonable rubrics would give a different order.

## 1. Immune reset with aHSCT in highly active relapsing MS

**The idea.** Wiping out and regrowing the immune system with aHSCT might stop inflammatory MS
activity for many years in people with highly active relapsing MS, and might allow some recovery of
disability.

**Chain of reasoning**

| # | Step | Evidence | Level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | In a trial of 110 people with relapsing-remitting MS who kept relapsing on drugs, disability progressed in 3 after aHSCT vs 34 after switching drugs (HR 0.07), and mean EDSS fell in the first year after aHSCT. Median follow-up was 2 years. The full text says the neurologist rating EDSS was masked but relapse recording was not, ocrelizumab and alemtuzumab were excluded from the comparison arm, and crossover after 1 year limits longer comparison. | [2019-ahsct-vs-drugs-trial-burt](../evidence/2019-ahsct-vs-drugs-trial-burt.md) (2) | 2 |
| 2 | In matched registry comparisons, aHSCT was associated with fewer relapses and more disability improvement than fingolimod, natalizumab, cladribine or alemtuzumab. | [2023-ahsct-vs-high-efficacy-drugs-kalincik](../evidence/2023-ahsct-vs-high-efficacy-drugs-kalincik.md) (3), [2026-ahsct-vs-cladribine-alemtuzumab-kalincik](../evidence/2026-ahsct-vs-cladribine-alemtuzumab-kalincik.md) (3), [2021-ahsct-vs-alemtuzumab-zhukovsky](../evidence/2021-ahsct-vs-alemtuzumab-zhukovsky.md) (3); Kebir 2026 meta-analysis in the [state-of-the-art finding, section 5](state-of-the-art-2026.md#5-cell-therapies-stem-cell-transplant-and-car-t) (3, very low certainty) | 3 |
| 3 | In single-arm cohorts, about two thirds of people with relapsing-remitting MS were free of disease activity, or of disability worsening, about 10 years after aHSCT. | [2024-ahsct-sweden-cohort-silfverberg](../evidence/2024-ahsct-sweden-cohort-silfverberg.md) (3), [2021-ahsct-long-term-outcomes-boffa](../evidence/2021-ahsct-long-term-outcomes-boffa.md) (3), [2017-ahsct-meta-analysis-sormani](../evidence/2017-ahsct-meta-analysis-sormani.md) (3) | 3 |
| 4 | Against ocrelizumab, today's common high-efficacy choice, the one matched comparison (110 people given aHSCT and 343 given ocrelizumab, 3 years) found no clear difference in relapses, worsening or improvement, with wide confidence intervals. So "better than the best current drugs" is not shown. | [2023-ahsct-vs-high-efficacy-drugs-kalincik](../evidence/2023-ahsct-vs-high-efficacy-drugs-kalincik.md) (3) | 3 |
| 5 | Outcomes were better in people who were younger, had relapsing rather than progressive MS and had less disability; in an older, mostly progressive cohort, 46% were free of progression at 5 years. | [2017-ahsct-long-term-outcomes-muraro](../evidence/2017-ahsct-long-term-outcomes-muraro.md) (3), [2017-ahsct-meta-analysis-sormani](../evidence/2017-ahsct-meta-analysis-sormani.md) (3) | 3 |
| 6 | Treatment-related deaths were about 2% to 3% in older and mixed cohorts and 0% to 0.6% in recent relapsing-remitting cohorts; severe short-term side effects (febrile neutropenia in 68%) and delayed complications (34%, mainly infections) were common. | [2017-ahsct-meta-analysis-sormani](../evidence/2017-ahsct-meta-analysis-sormani.md) (3), [2017-ahsct-long-term-outcomes-muraro](../evidence/2017-ahsct-long-term-outcomes-muraro.md) (3), [2021-ahsct-long-term-outcomes-boffa](../evidence/2021-ahsct-long-term-outcomes-boffa.md) (3), [2024-ahsct-sweden-cohort-silfverberg](../evidence/2024-ahsct-sweden-cohort-silfverberg.md) (3), [2026-ahsct-vs-cladribine-alemtuzumab-kalincik](../evidence/2026-ahsct-vs-cladribine-alemtuzumab-kalincik.md) (3) | 3 |

**Weakest link.** Step 4 (level 3, and a null result with wide uncertainty). The hypothesis that
aHSCT "stops" MS better than today's best drugs rests on one short registry comparison with
ocrelizumab (110 aHSCT and 343 ocrelizumab participants matched, 3 years) that found no clear difference, plus two small randomized trials: MIST (step 1), whose comparison drugs
date from 2005 to 2016, and ASTIMS (21 people, aHSCT vs mitoxantrone; less MRI activity, no
difference in disability progression; Mancardi GL et al., *Neurology* 2015,
[doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000001329](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000001329), PMID
[25672923](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25672923/)). Neither was a dedicated comparison with today's most-used high-efficacy drugs: MIST's drug arm
included natalizumab (21 people) but excluded ocrelizumab and alemtuzumab, and ASTIMS compared
with mitoxantrone. **What would break it:** randomized trials against current high-efficacy
drugs, with blinded disability ratings, finding no advantage; or late harms (cancers, other
autoimmune disease, fertility) that the current files barely cover turning out to be common.

**Already being tested.** RAM-MS, aHSCT vs alemtuzumab, cladribine or ocrelizumab
([NCT03477500](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03477500), about 100 people); BEAT-MS, aHSCT vs
best available therapy ([NCT04047628](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04047628), about 156
people). Also regimen and safety studies
([NCT05832515](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05832515),
[NCT00716066](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00716066)).

**Next trial.** People with highly active relapsing-remitting MS despite treatment; aHSCT vs an
anti-CD20 antibody; main outcome time to 6-month confirmed disability worsening, with confirmed
disability improvement as a key secondary outcome, rated by assessors who do not know the treatment;
at least 5 years of follow-up with full counting of late harms. Roughly 200 to 300 people (a rough
judgment, not a power calculation). If RAM-MS and BEAT-MS report clearly, this trial may not be
needed.

**Score: 8** (A 2, B 1 of 6 steps, C 2, D 3).

## 2. Two compartments: B-cell depletion plus a brain-penetrant BTK inhibitor

**The idea.** Anti-CD20 drugs control inflammation that enters the brain from the blood, but
worsening without relapses may come from inflammation trapped behind the blood-brain barrier, seen as
smoldering (rim or slowly expanding) lesions. Adding a BTK inhibitor that reaches the brain might
calm that second compartment and slow worsening that anti-CD20 drugs alone do not stop.

**Chain of reasoning**

| # | Step | Evidence | Level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Most confirmed disability worsening in relapsing MS happens without relapses (PIRA): at least half in a systematic review of cohorts, and 72.3% of events in 16,130 people in the Italian registry. | [2023-pira-definitions-muller](../evidence/2023-pira-definitions-muller.md) (3), [2024-pira-frequency-portaccio](../evidence/2024-pira-frequency-portaccio.md) (3); Kappos 2020 pooled analysis in the [state-of-the-art finding, section 2](state-of-the-art-2026.md#2-current-disease-modifying-therapies-what-they-do-and-dont-do) (3) | 3 |
| 2 | Peripheral B-cell depletion leaves a residual: in a trial of 1,013 people with primary progressive MS, ocrelizumab slowed but did not stop progression (33% vs 40%); in two trials (860 and 753 people), a higher dose did not slow progression more; in 10,499 people in the French registry, starting a high-efficacy drug was not associated with less worsening independent of relapses and MRI activity. | [2026-ocrelizumab-ppms-giovannoni](../evidence/2026-ocrelizumab-ppms-giovannoni.md) (2), [2026-ocrelizumab-high-dose-hauser](../evidence/2026-ocrelizumab-high-dose-hauser.md) (2), [2026-high-efficacy-therapy-pirma-rollot](../evidence/2026-high-efficacy-therapy-pirma-rollot.md) (3) | 2 |
| 3 | People with paramagnetic rim lesions were more likely to have PIRA (HR 1.8) and, in exploratory analyses, more spinal fluid signs of inflammation inside the CNS; more slowly expanding lesion volume and more rim lesions were associated with tissue loss and worsening. | [2026-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-lauerer](../evidence/2026-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-lauerer.md) (3), [2022-slowly-expanding-lesions-calvi](../evidence/2022-slowly-expanding-lesions-calvi.md) (3), [2024-spinal-cord-atrophy-cagol](../evidence/2024-spinal-cord-atrophy-cagol.md) (3) | 3 |
| 4 | MS risk genes are enriched in microglia; it has been proposed that microglia help direct the disease within the CNS. | [2019-genomic-map-imsgc](../evidence/2019-genomic-map-imsgc.md) (3 for the association; the microglia role is a proposal) | 3 |
| 5 | In a trial of 1,131 people with non-relapsing secondary progressive MS, the brain-penetrant BTK inhibitor tolebrutinib lowered 6-month confirmed progression (22.6% vs 30.7%, HR 0.69). It showed no statistically significant difference in relapses vs teriflunomide in relapsing MS, and the trial did not show which mechanism was responsible. | [2025-tolebrutinib-hercules-fox](../evidence/2025-tolebrutinib-hercules-fox.md) (2), [2025-tolebrutinib-gemini-oh](../evidence/2025-tolebrutinib-gemini-oh.md) (2) | 2 |
| 6 | Reducing rim or slowly expanding lesions slows PIRA. | Not shown. Treatments can change these lesions: in ORATORIO, ocrelizumab reduced chronic activity inside old lesions, which predicted progression in placebo-treated people only, and no mediation analysis is reported; in a post hoc phase 2 analysis, high-dose evobrutinib lowered slowly expanding lesion volume, and evobrutinib then failed in phase 3; ibudilast went with less slowly enlarging lesion volume in a post hoc SPRINT-MS analysis; observational results are mixed: B-cell depletion was associated with less lesion tissue expansion in one contrast (not in the overall treatment test), but no rim lesions disappeared over 2 years on anti-CD20 drugs, and two small aHSCT studies had different findings. None shows that the lesion change is what slows worsening ([questions/pira.md](../questions/pira.md)). | none for the step itself ([2019-ocrelizumab-chronic-lesion-activity-elliott](../evidence/2019-ocrelizumab-chronic-lesion-activity-elliott.md) (2/3), [2024-evobrutinib-slowly-expanding-lesions-arnold](../evidence/2024-evobrutinib-slowly-expanding-lesions-arnold.md) (2), [2024-ibudilast-slowly-enlarging-lesions-nakamura](../evidence/2024-ibudilast-slowly-enlarging-lesions-nakamura.md) (2), [2023-anti-cd20-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-maggi](../evidence/2023-anti-cd20-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-maggi.md) (3), [2026-ahsct-rim-lesions-7t-amin](../evidence/2026-ahsct-rim-lesions-7t-amin.md) (4), [2026-dmt-chronic-lesion-expansion-guilfoyle](../evidence/2026-dmt-chronic-lesion-expansion-guilfoyle.md) (3), [2026-ahsct-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-mariottini](../evidence/2026-ahsct-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-mariottini.md) (4) show lesion changes only) |
| 7 | A BTK inhibitor added to an anti-CD20 drug slows progression more than the anti-CD20 drug alone. | None. No file reports this combination. | none |
| 8 | Safety: in HERCULES, serious adverse events were 15.0% vs 10.4% and ALT over 3 times normal 4.0% vs 1.6%; in pooled trial safety data on 2,958 people, 6 met Hy's law criteria, with one liver transplant followed by death. Evobrutinib also raised liver enzymes more than teriflunomide. | [2025-tolebrutinib-hercules-fox](../evidence/2025-tolebrutinib-hercules-fox.md) (2); Maxwell 2026 (4) and evolutionRMS (2) in the [state-of-the-art finding, section 3](state-of-the-art-2026.md#3-btk-inhibitors-and-other-late-stage-therapies-2024-2026) | 2 |

**Weakest link.** Steps 6 and 7 (no evidence). HERCULES shows a BTK inhibitor alone slowed
progression in one population; nothing yet shows that rim lesions are the target that matters, or
that the drug adds anything on top of B-cell depletion. Step 1 also has a known soft spot: a
reanalysis of two natalizumab trials found many "PIRA" events came with new MRI activity and that
improvement events outnumbered PIRA, raising doubts about PIRA as a clean outcome
([2025-pira-trial-outcome-strijbis](../evidence/2025-pira-trial-outcome-strijbis.md), level 3).
**What would break it:** an add-on trial in people on anti-CD20 drugs showing no extra effect on
confirmed progression; a BTK inhibitor that clears rim lesions without changing worsening; a second
placebo-controlled progressive trial failing to repeat HERCULES; or liver harm that outweighs a
modest benefit.

**One of those checks has partly happened already (as of 11/03/2026).** A peer-reviewed overview of
the tolebrutinib program reports that tolebrutinib reduced disability progression by 31% in
non-relapsing secondary progressive MS (HERCULES) and 29% in relapsing MS (the relapsing GEMINI result was nominal: the trials report no formal hypothesis test for it under their prespecified testing hierarchy, so it is exploratory), but showed "no meaningful
benefit" in PERSEUS (Maxwell CR et al., *Mult Scler Relat Disord* 2026,
[doi:10.1016/j.msard.2026.107404](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.msard.2026.107404), PMID
[42546561](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42546561/); also cited in the
[state-of-the-art finding, section 3](state-of-the-art-2026.md#3-btk-inhibitors-and-other-late-stage-therapies-2024-2026)).
This is a summary inside a safety paper, level 4 here; no peer-reviewed report of PERSEUS itself is
in these files, and its outcome numbers were not checked here. Two cautions on what it means:
PERSEUS was in **primary progressive MS** and HERCULES in **non-relapsing secondary progressive MS**,
so this is a different population rather than a failed repeat of HERCULES; and neither trial added a
BTK inhibitor to an anti-CD20 drug, so it does not test the combination this hypothesis is about. It
does weaken the broader idea that a brain-penetrant BTK inhibitor slows progression across
progressive MS, and it is a reason not to assume the HERCULES effect carries over.

**Already being tested.** BTK inhibitors against placebo on disability in progressive MS:
orelabrutinib ([NCT07067463](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07067463),
[NCT07299019](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07299019)) and remibrutinib (REMASTER,
[NCT07225504](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07225504)). Fenebrutinib against ocrelizumab in
primary progressive MS (FENtrepid, [NCT04544449](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04544449); a
head-to-head comparison, not an add-on). Rim lesions as the outcome: rocbrutinib vs dimethyl
fumarate ([NCT07776743](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07776743)) and remibrutinib, single arm
([NCT07222956](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07222956)). Switching from ocrelizumab to
remibrutinib ([NCT06846281](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06846281)) and long-term tolebrutinib
safety ([NCT06372145](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06372145)). No trial in the tracker adds a
BTK inhibitor to an anti-CD20 drug. No full peer-reviewed fenebrutinib phase 3 trial report was available
when the state-of-the-art finding was written, and a PubMed search on 11/03/2026 still found none.
FENtrepid has been presented at conferences, with an abstract in a journal supplement, but that is
not a full trial report.
Remibrutinib beat teriflunomide on relapses in relapsing MS (REMODEL-1 and 2,
[2026-remibrutinib-remodel-wiendl](../evidence/2026-remibrutinib-remodel-wiendl.md), level 2), so not
every BTK inhibitor fails on relapses. But it was not an add-on to an anti-CD20 drug, and its
pooled disability results (6-month HR 0.70, nominal) measure overall progression, not PIRA, so it
does not test steps 6 or 7.

**Next trial.** Adults with non-relapsing progressive MS who have been on an anti-CD20 drug for at
least a year and have at least one paramagnetic rim lesion; a brain-penetrant BTK inhibitor vs
placebo, both added to continued anti-CD20 treatment; main outcome time to 6-month confirmed
composite disability progression (EDSS, timed 25-foot walk, 9-hole peg test), with change in rim
lesions, slowly expanding lesion volume and serum GFAP as secondary outcomes, and close liver
monitoring. An add-on effect is likely smaller than HERCULES's effect against placebo, so roughly
1,000 or more people over 2 to 3 years. Selecting people by high GFAP might shrink that: the study
authors estimated a cut of about 20% in PIRA trial size. That figure is **a modeling estimate, level
5 in this repo's scale**, not a tested trial design, and it should not be mixed up with the
**observational association it is built on, level 3**: a GFAP Z score above 1.0 went with a higher
risk of PIRA in the next year or so (hazard ratio 1.45, 95% CI 1.21-1.75 in the Swiss cohort; 1.36,
95% CI 1.07-1.71 in EPIC)
([2026-gfap-dynamics-pira-einsiedler](../evidence/2026-gfap-dynamics-pira-einsiedler.md)).
A phase 2 step of about 100 to 150 people, with rim-lesion change at 1 to 2 years, could come first.

**Score: 7** (A 0, B 3 of 8 steps, C 2, D 3).

## 3. Nerve protection added to anti-inflammatory treatment in progressive MS

**The idea.** In progressive MS, much of the worsening may come from nerve fibers being lost after
inflammation is partly controlled. A drug that protects nerve tissue, given alongside
anti-inflammatory treatment, might slow that loss and the disability that goes with it.

**Chain of reasoning**

| # | Step | Evidence | Level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Worsening without relapses was associated with tissue loss: faster spinal cord atrophy in people with PIRA, and more slowly expanding lesion volume going with more brain volume loss and progression. | [2024-spinal-cord-atrophy-cagol](../evidence/2024-spinal-cord-atrophy-cagol.md) (3), [2022-slowly-expanding-lesions-calvi](../evidence/2022-slowly-expanding-lesions-calvi.md) (3) | 3 |
| 2 | Anti-inflammatory treatment leaves a residual: ocrelizumab slowed but did not stop progression in a trial of 1,013 people; high-efficacy first treatment was not associated with less worsening independent of relapses and MRI. | [2026-ocrelizumab-ppms-giovannoni](../evidence/2026-ocrelizumab-ppms-giovannoni.md) (2), [2026-high-efficacy-therapy-pirma-rollot](../evidence/2026-high-efficacy-therapy-pirma-rollot.md) (3) | 2 |
| 3 | A drug can slow brain tissue loss: in a trial of 255 people with progressive MS, ibudilast slowed brain atrophy on MRI (about 2.5 ml less over 96 weeks); no disability effect was reported in the abstract. | [2018-ibudilast-sprint-ms-fox](../evidence/2018-ibudilast-sprint-ms-fox.md) (2) | 2 |
| 4 | Slowing tissue loss slows disability people can feel. | None in support. Against it: in a trial of 964 people, simvastatin did not slow progression after a positive phase 2 atrophy result; lipoic acid (115 people) and high-dose biotin (642 people) did not help walking or disability. | none (contradicted by [2025-simvastatin-ms-stat2-chataway](../evidence/2025-simvastatin-ms-stat2-chataway.md) (2), [2026-lipoic-acid-progressive-spain](../evidence/2026-lipoic-acid-progressive-spain.md) (2), [2020-high-dose-biotin-spi2-cree](../evidence/2020-high-dose-biotin-spi2-cree.md) (2)) |

**Weakest link.** Step 4 (no supporting evidence, and three negative level 2 trials). The jump from
an MRI signal to a clinical benefit has already failed once (simvastatin). Ibudilast's atrophy signal
has not been tested on disability in a phase 3 trial in these files. **What would break it:** a
large trial of a candidate with an MRI signal (such as ibudilast) showing no effect on confirmed
progression; or evidence that atrophy in progressive MS is driven mainly by ongoing compartmentalized
inflammation, which would point back to hypothesis 2 instead.

**Already being tested.** Nicotinamide riboside on disability worsening (Norseman,
[NCT05740722](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05740722), about 300 people); N-acetyl cysteine on
brain and cord atrophy ([NCT05122559](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05122559)); NLY01, a GLP-1
drug, on brain atrophy (TAG-MS, [NCT07497399](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07497399));
metformin in older people with MS ([NCT06463743](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06463743)).

**Next trial.** People with progressive MS on stable anti-inflammatory treatment; a candidate that
slowed atrophy in phase 2 vs placebo, ideally as one arm of a multi-arm trial so several candidates
share a placebo group; main outcome time to 6-month confirmed composite disability progression, not
atrophy. MS-STAT2 needed 964 people for one comparison, so roughly 900 to 1,000 people per
comparison over 2 to 4 years.

**Score: 6** (A 0, B 2 of 4 steps, C 2, D 2).

## 4. Repair after control: remyelination on top of effective immune treatment

**The idea.** Once new inflammation is controlled, a drug that helps the brain rebuild myelin might
restore some lost function, so that disability improves rather than only stops getting worse.

**Chain of reasoning**

| # | Step | Evidence | Level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Trials together show that natalizumab, cladribine and alemtuzumab reduce relapses over 2 years; anti-CD20 antibodies reduced relapses and new lesions in trials. So new inflammation can be largely controlled in relapsing MS. | Gonzalez-Lorenzo 2024 Cochrane review (1) and OPERA I and II (2) in the [state-of-the-art finding, section 2](state-of-the-art-2026.md#2-current-disease-modifying-therapies-what-they-do-and-dont-do) | 1 |
| 2 | A drug can change a measure of myelin in people: in a trial of 50 people with relapsing MS and old optic nerve damage, clemastine shortened the visual signal delay by 1.7 ms per eye, and myelin water fraction rose in the corpus callosum while people took it. | [2017-clemastine-rebuild-green](../evidence/2017-clemastine-rebuild-green.md) (2), [2023-myelin-water-imaging-caverzasi](../evidence/2023-myelin-water-imaging-caverzasi.md) (2) | 2 |
| 3 | A change in a repair measure leads to function people can feel. | None. No file links a treatment-induced change in VEP latency or myelin imaging to later function in the same people ([questions/repair.md](../questions/repair.md) lists this as a gap). Two years after RENEW, VEP latency was numerically but not significantly better with opicinumab, with no reported benefit on vision or other function in 52 people ([2025-opicinumab-renewed-aktas](../evidence/2025-opicinumab-renewed-aktas.md), 2); too small to settle the question. Against the broader idea: opicinumab added to standard treatment did not meet its disability outcome in two trials (419 and 263 people), and bexarotene did not change its MRI myelin outcome. | none (contradicted by [2019-opicinumab-synergy-cadavid](../evidence/2019-opicinumab-synergy-cadavid.md) (2), [2026-opicinumab-affinity-calabresi](../evidence/2026-opicinumab-affinity-calabresi.md) (2), [2021-bexarotene-ccmr-one-brown](../evidence/2021-bexarotene-ccmr-one-brown.md) (2)) |
| 4 | Safety: no serious adverse events in 50 people with relapsing MS on clemastine, but in progressive MS a clemastine arm was stopped when 3 of 9 met safety stopping criteria and worsened faster; in cells, clemastine with ATP triggered pyroptosis. | [2017-clemastine-rebuild-green](../evidence/2017-clemastine-rebuild-green.md) (2), [2025-clemastine-trap-ms-kocot](../evidence/2025-clemastine-trap-ms-kocot.md) (4 clinical, 5 cells) | 4 for the progressive MS signal |

**Weakest link.** Step 3 (no evidence). The only positive repair trial changed a nerve-signal test
in 50 people; the two add-on trials that measured disability (opicinumab) were negative. The
clemastine harm signal in progressive MS (step 4) is small and not randomized, but it is
unresolved. **What would break it:** a well-powered trial in which a drug clearly improves a myelin
measure but not function; or confirmation that remyelinating drugs cause harm in some settings.

**Already being tested.** Clemastine after acute optic neuritis (ReCOVER,
[NCT02521311](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02521311)); ifenprodil on VEP latency (MODIF-MS,
[NCT06330077](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06330077)); metformin as add-on, on walking speed
([NCT05893225](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05893225)); natalizumab on remyelination at first
presentation (AttackMS, [NCT05418010](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05418010)); testosterone
(TOTEM-RRMS, [NCT03910738](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03910738)); mesenchymal stem cells on
evoked potentials (SMART-MS, [NCT04749667](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04749667)).

**Next trial.** People with relapsing MS whose inflammation is controlled on a high-efficacy drug and
who have a chronic, measurable deficit in one pathway (for example old optic nerve damage); a
candidate repair drug vs placebo, added to their immune treatment; main outcome a function people
notice in that pathway (for example low-contrast vision) at 12 months, with VEP latency and myelin
water fraction as secondary outcomes so the trial also tests whether those measures track function.
Roughly 150 to 250 people. People with progressive MS would be included only after safety is clearer
there.

**Score: 5** (A 0, B 2 of 4 steps, C 1, D 2).

## 5. Deep B-cell and plasma-cell removal inside the CNS with CAR-T

**The idea.** Anti-CD20 drugs remove B cells in the blood but may miss antibody-making cells that
have settled inside the brain and spinal cord. CAR-T cells aimed at CD19 or BCMA can enter the spinal
fluid and might remove those cells, quieting inflammation that drives progression.

**Chain of reasoning**

| # | Step | Evidence | Level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Inflammation inside the CNS persists in some people: people with rim lesions had more spinal fluid signs of long-lasting CNS inflammation (exploratory). | [2026-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-lauerer](../evidence/2026-paramagnetic-rim-lesions-lauerer.md) (3) | 3 |
| 2 | More peripheral B-cell depletion did not help more: in two trials, a higher ocrelizumab dose did not slow progression more than the standard dose. The trials were not designed to say why. (The CAR-T paper's background states that anti-CD20 drugs do not remove plasma cells well; that is context, not a tested finding in these files.) | [2026-ocrelizumab-high-dose-hauser](../evidence/2026-ocrelizumab-high-dose-hauser.md) (2) | 2 |
| 3 | Reported in 2 cases, CD19 CAR-T cells reached the spinal fluid without neurotoxicity, and antibody production in spinal fluid fell in one. | [2024-cd19-car-t-fischbach](../evidence/2024-cd19-car-t-fischbach.md) (4) | 4 |
| 4 | Reported in 5 cases, anti-BCMA CAR-T depleted plasma cells in CNS compartments and reduced markers of microglial activation. | [2025-bcma-car-t-qin](../evidence/2025-bcma-car-t-qin.md) (4) | 4 |
| 5 | These changes slow or reverse disability. | In Qin's 5 people, only exploratory, uncontrolled measures, which cannot show benefit. A peer-reviewed review of the Qin full text reports that EDSS and Nine-Hole Peg Test scores improved in all 5, with 3 to 9 months of follow-up (Bradhurst P, Hardy TA, Riminton S, *BMJ Neurol Open* 2026, [doi:10.1136/bmjno-2026-001611](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjno-2026-001611), PMID [42824951](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42824951/)). There was no control group and no blinding, and improvement in an open-label handful of people is what a placebo-controlled trial exists to rule out. A reviewer reports that the indexed full text also lists timed-walk changes among exploratory endpoints and describes 1 primary progressive and 4 secondary progressive patients, matching the abstract; a reviewer has since seen these exploratory outcomes in a [manufacturer-hosted corrected proof](https://en.iasobio.com/uploads/2025-10/17/_1760664636_3659.pdf), though its exact values are inconsistent between versions, and the PubMed abstract itself gives no clinical outcomes. These are reported improvements, not evidence that the treatment caused them. A further company-funded report describes 2 people with secondary progressive MS on a CD20/BCMA bispecific CAR-T whose EDSS went from 4.0 to 3.0 and 6.5 to 6.0 over 12 and 6 months, also uncontrolled. | [2025-bcma-car-t-qin](../evidence/2025-bcma-car-t-qin.md) (4), with outcomes as relayed by the Bradhurst 2026 review (4); [2026-cd20-bcma-bispecific-car-t-ling](../evidence/2026-cd20-bcma-bispecific-car-t-ling.md) (4) | 4 |
| 6 | Safety: only mild cytokine release syndrome in 5 people, but severe low blood counts in all 5 within 40 days; no neurotoxicity in 2. | [2025-bcma-car-t-qin](../evidence/2025-bcma-car-t-qin.md) (4), [2024-cd19-car-t-fischbach](../evidence/2024-cd19-car-t-fischbach.md) (4) | 4 |

**Weakest link.** Steps 3 to 6, all level 4. Nine people in the three reports with files here, with no
comparison group anywhere in the chain. Step 5 is no longer empty: clinical measures were recorded
in 7 people (5 in Qin, 2 in Ling) and improved. But uncontrolled, unblinded measures over 3 to 12 months in 7 people cannot
show that the treatment helps. The Qin values are inconsistent between versions, so its file quotes
none, and the Ling report is company-funded. The step is scored 4 (case series), not higher.
**What would break it:** early trials showing CNS plasma cells deplete but disability keeps
worsening at the same rate, a controlled trial in which the open-label improvement does not repeat,
or a safety profile (cytopenias, infections) that is too heavy for a slowly progressing illness.

**Already being tested.** Many early-phase trials, mostly single-arm and safety-first, including
KYSA-7, anti-CD19 CAR-T in refractory progressive MS
([NCT06384976](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06384976), phase 2, about 120 people);
[NCT06451159](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06451159);
[NCT06138132](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06138132); the BCMA trial behind the Qin report
([NCT04561557](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04561557)); CD20/BCMA CAR-T in progressive MS with
confirmed worsening as a measure ([NCT07825389](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07825389));
rapcabtagene autoleucel in progressive and relapsing MS
([NCT06675864](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06675864),
[NCT06617793](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06617793)); and others in section D of the tracker.

**Next trial.** People with progressive MS still worsening despite an anti-CD20 drug, with signs of
antibody production inside the CNS; CAR-T vs continued anti-CD20 treatment, with the control group
offered CAR-T after the main outcome (delayed start); main outcome confirmed composite disability
progression at 2 years, with slowly expanding lesions and spinal fluid markers as secondary outcomes.
Roughly 60 to 120 people as a randomized phase 2 step, before any larger trial.

**Score: 5** (A 1, B 1 of 6 steps, C 1, D 2). A rose from 0 to 1 because the weakest step is now
level 4 rather than no evidence. Nothing else in the chain changed, and the new measures are
uncontrolled.

## 6. Controlling EBV in people who already have MS

**The idea.** If ongoing EBV activity, or EBV-infected B cells, keep driving the immune attack, then
an antiviral or EBV-targeted T cells, alone or alongside B-cell depletion, might quiet MS.

**Chain of reasoning**

| # | Step | Evidence | Level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | EBV infection comes before MS and is strongly associated with it: MS risk was 32 times higher after EBV infection in more than 10 million US military personnel; mononucleosis was associated with about 3 times the risk; EBV antibody changes appeared more than 20 years before onset. | [2022-epstein-barr-virus-bjornevik](../evidence/2022-epstein-barr-virus-bjornevik.md) (3), [2024-mononucleosis-cohort-england-goldacre](../evidence/2024-mononucleosis-cohort-england-goldacre.md) (3), [2026-mononucleosis-lab-confirmed-st-sauver](../evidence/2026-mononucleosis-lab-confirmed-st-sauver.md) (3); Grut 2026 in the [state-of-the-art finding, section 1](state-of-the-art-2026.md#1-what-causes-ms-especially-epstein-barr-virus) (3) | 3 |
| 2 | Possible mechanisms: an MS spinal fluid antibody bound both EBNA1 and the brain protein GlialCAM, and EBNA1 immunization worsened disease in mice; in cells, certain NK and EBV-specific T cells killed GlialCAM-reactive cells. | [2022-ebna1-glialcam-mimicry-lanz](../evidence/2022-ebna1-glialcam-mimicry-lanz.md) (5), [2023-ebv-immune-control-vietzen](../evidence/2023-ebv-immune-control-vietzen.md) (5) | 5 |
| 3 | Ongoing EBV activity keeps MS active after it has started. | Not shown. Reported in 1 case: no relapses for nearly five years on tenofovir-containing HIV treatment, with activity returning after a switch. **EBV was never measured in this case**, so it cannot show that EBV activity is what kept MS quiet or active: the person was being treated for HIV, the drugs act on other targets, and a single uncontrolled case cannot separate the drug from the natural course of the illness. It is consistent with the step, not support for it. | [2024-tenofovir-case-report-torkildsen](../evidence/2024-tenofovir-case-report-torkildsen.md) (a level 4 case, consistent with the idea but not support for it); none in support |
| 4 | An antiviral lowers EBV activity in people with MS. | **Not established, and mixed by drug and compartment.** Two files point in different directions. In samples from a valacyclovir trial, the share of saliva samples with EBV DNA was lower on valacyclovir (9%; the abstract also gives an overall 41%, whose comparison group and denominator are unclear; percentages of samples, not people), with no significant change in plasma and no clinical benefit shown. Its link to the parent trial is unconfirmed. In an open-label pilot of 30 people on natalizumab, famciclovir did not significantly lower EBV in saliva. Neither shows a lower EBV level changing MS. The older randomized antiviral trials in MS (acyclovir, valacyclovir) were not designed specifically around EBV and did not establish an EBV-mediated benefit; see [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md). | [2005-ebv-hhv6-dna-valacyclovir-hollsberg](../evidence/2005-ebv-hhv6-dna-valacyclovir-hollsberg.md) (4) for saliva only; [2024-famciclovir-ebv-shedding-dobson](../evidence/2024-famciclovir-ebv-shedding-dobson.md) (4) against |
| 5 | Improvement follows EBV-targeted T cells (uncontrolled observations only). | Reported in 10 people with progressive MS in an open-label phase 1 trial, 7 showed some improvement with no control group. A placebo-controlled phase 2 trial was reported by its sponsor not to have met its main outcome, but has no peer-reviewed paper, so it is not evidence here. | [2018-ebv-specific-t-cells-pender](../evidence/2018-ebv-specific-t-cells-pender.md) (4) |
| 6 | Lowering EBV activity changes relapses or disability. | None. An expert panel shortlisted licensed anti-EBV drugs for trials; that is a proposal, not efficacy. | none ([2025-anti-ebv-drug-repurposing-li](../evidence/2025-anti-ebv-drug-repurposing-li.md) (6) proposes testing) |

**Weakest link.** Steps 4 and 6 (for step 4, a valacyclovir substudy found less EBV DNA in saliva only and the
famciclovir pilot found no significant drop, both level 4; step 6 has no evidence), and step 3 has no support either (one level 4 case that never measured EBV); steps 2 and 5 rest on level 5 and level 4. The strong level 3 association is about getting
MS, not about what keeps it going. Only two files bear, weakly, on whether
B-cell depletion already changes EBV activity itself, which matters for any "on top of anti-CD20"
version.
Six files show that anti-CD20 drugs go with lower EBV-specific T-cell responses and changes in EBV
antibodies; one found blood EBV DNA too rarely to judge, and one found no EBV DNA in saliva in five
treated people. None shows that treatment-induced
changes improve MS outcomes (see [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md)).
**What would break it:** a placebo-controlled trial in which an antiviral clearly lowers EBV activity
but MS outcomes do not change.

**Already being tested.** Tenofovir/emtricitabine on EBV levels in saliva and blood
([NCT05957913](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05957913), about 50 people); a person's own
EBV-specific T cells after a first episode suggestive of MS
([NCT02912897](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02912897), about 7 people). A multi-stage
placebo-controlled trial of famciclovir and spironolactone in progressive MS (STOP-MS) has a
published protocol, described in [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md), but is not in the tracker.

**Next trial.** People with relapsing MS; a licensed anti-EBV drug vs placebo, added to usual
treatment, in two stages: first, about 100 to 150 people with a measure of EBV activity as the main
outcome, to show the drug hits its target (which the famciclovir pilot did not); then, if it does,
about 300 or more people with confirmed disability progression or relapses as the main outcome.

**Score: 3** (A 0, B 0 of 6 steps, C 1, D 2).

## 7. Preventing EBV infection to prevent MS

**The idea.** If EBV infection, especially a late first infection that causes mononucleosis, is a
necessary step toward MS, a vaccine that prevents infection might prevent many cases. This would
stop MS before it starts rather than in people who have it.

**Chain of reasoning**

| # | Step | Evidence | Level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | MS risk was 32 times higher after EBV infection, with no rise after cytomegalovirus, and the nerve-damage marker NfL rose only after EBV infection. | [2022-epstein-barr-virus-bjornevik](../evidence/2022-epstein-barr-virus-bjornevik.md) (3) | 3 |
| 2 | People with mononucleosis were about 3 times as likely to develop MS later. | [2024-mononucleosis-cohort-england-goldacre](../evidence/2024-mononucleosis-cohort-england-goldacre.md) (3), [2026-mononucleosis-lab-confirmed-st-sauver](../evidence/2026-mononucleosis-lab-confirmed-st-sauver.md) (3) | 3 |
| 3 | Later first infection may matter: in 2.6 million Danes, having siblings close in age was associated with lower risk of both mononucleosis and MS, in matching patterns. | [2023-siblings-delayed-ebv-rostgaard](../evidence/2023-siblings-delayed-ebv-rostgaard.md) (3) | 3 |
| 4 | A vaccine can reduce mononucleosis: in a trial of 181 EBV-negative young adults, a gp350 vaccine had an estimated 78% efficacy against mononucleosis (95% CI 1% to 96%) but did not prevent infection. | [2007-ebv-gp350-vaccine-sokal](../evidence/2007-ebv-gp350-vaccine-sokal.md) (2; MS not measured) | 2 |
| 5 | Preventing EBV infection, or mononucleosis, lowers MS risk. | None. It has been proposed (Rostgaard and colleagues suggest an early vaccine might nearly eliminate MS) but never tested. | none |

**Weakest link.** Step 5 (no evidence), and no vaccine in these files has been shown to prevent
infection. A newer gp350 nanoparticle vaccine raised antibodies in an open-label phase 1 trial, and no
seronegative person was infected after completing three doses, through day 540, but with no control group that cannot show
prevention ([2026-ebv-gp350-ferritin-vaccine-durkee-shock](../evidence/2026-ebv-gp350-ferritin-vaccine-durkee-shock.md), level 4).
Most adults carry EBV and most never get MS, so other factors (genes, vitamin D, smoking, adolescent
obesity; see [questions/clustering.md](../questions/clustering.md)) also matter. **What would break
it:** a vaccine that prevents infection in a large trial, followed over many years, with no fall in
MS; or evidence that preventing only mononucleosis, not infection, leaves MS risk unchanged.

**Already being tested.** The MS trial tracker covers MS trials only, so prevention trials in
healthy people are listed in [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md): a completed, small,
placebo-controlled gp350-ferritin trial with infection endpoints
([NCT05683834](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05683834), no results posted) and phase 1 or 1/2
mRNA and nanoparticle trials. In MS itself, one trial gives mRNA-1195, an investigational EBV
vaccine, to EBV-positive people with MS ([NCT06735248](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06735248));
that tests treatment, not prevention.

**Next trial.** EBV-negative adolescents; a vaccine designed to prevent infection vs placebo; main
outcome EBV infection (seroconversion) and mononucleosis over a few years, with consent to long-term
registry follow-up for MS. Showing an effect on MS itself would likely need tens of thousands of
people followed for 15 years or more, which is why this ranks last on "testable soon" despite its
large potential reach.

**Score: 2** (A 0, B 1 of 5 steps, C 1, D 0).

## Where it disagrees, and what's missing

- **The scores mostly measure how far each idea has been tested, not how promising it is.** Five of
  seven hypotheses score 0 on the weakest link because their final step (does it change outcomes
  people feel?) has never been tested in a way that could show it. EBV prevention and control rank low partly because the
  decisive trials are long and hard, not because the evidence points against them.
- **Negative level 2 evidence sits under hypotheses 3 and 4.** Simvastatin, lipoic acid, biotin and
  opicinumab all failed on clinical outcomes. These ideas survive only as "a different drug or a
  better-chosen population might work."
- **PIRA as an outcome is not settled.** Definitions vary widely and PIRA often comes with MRI
  activity ([2023-pira-definitions-muller](../evidence/2023-pira-definitions-muller.md),
  [2025-pira-trial-outcome-strijbis](../evidence/2025-pira-trial-outcome-strijbis.md), both level
  3), which affects hypotheses 2 and 3.
- **"No file here" is not the same as "nobody has studied it."** The list below is about this
  repo's evidence files. Some of these gaps are also gaps in the literature as far as this review
  found: no trial adding a BTK inhibitor to an anti-CD20 drug, no published results showing that a
  vaccine prevents EBV infection (a small placebo-controlled trial with infection outcomes,
  [NCT05683834](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05683834), is completed with no results posted), and no randomized CAR-T trial in MS that has reported disability outcomes yet (one is
  registered: KYSA-7, [NCT06384976](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06384976), a randomized phase 2
  trial of the CAR-T KYV-101 against an anti-CD20 antibody, with confirmed disability progression
  as its primary outcome; active, not recruiting, no results posted as of 11/03/2026). Others are not gaps in
  the literature at all, only missing files here (see the antiviral leads below).
- **Thin spots in the evidence files:**
  - Little evidence on whether B-cell depletion changes EBV activity itself, and none on whether that
    relates to its effect on MS (hypotheses 5 and 6). Six files now cover this on anti-CD20 drugs; two measured EBV DNA
    (rarely found in blood at all; no salivary shedding in five treated people), and none showed that treatment-induced changes improve outcomes.
  - No file showing that treating rim or slowly expanding lesions slows PIRA (hypothesis 2). Seven
    files now cover treatment and these lesions, with mixed results; none tests whether that explains slower
    worsening.
  - No file with BTK inhibitor and anti-CD20 combination data, or a full peer-reviewed fenebrutinib
    phase 3 trial report (hypothesis 2). A PubMed search on 11/03/2026 found neither (FENtrepid has
    only conference abstracts and presentations); REMODEL (remibrutinib vs
    teriflunomide) is now a file but does not fill this gap.
  - No file linking a treatment-induced change in a repair measure (VEP, myelin water fraction) to
    later function in the same people (hypothesis 4). RENEWED, Schlaeger 2014 and Öncel 2026 are now files, and none
    closes it (see [questions/repair.md](../questions/repair.md)).
  - No CAR-T report with a *controlled* clinical outcome; 9 people in the three reports with files here
    (other single cases exist), and the only clinical measures are exploratory and uncontrolled (hypothesis 5). Ling 2026 adds 2
    company-funded cases with small EDSS changes over 6 to 12 months. The Qin paper reports
    those measures (seen by a reviewer in a [manufacturer-hosted corrected proof](https://en.iasobio.com/uploads/2025-10/17/_1760664636_3659.pdf)), but its exact values are
    inconsistent between versions, so the evidence file quotes none; its abstract gives no clinical outcomes.
  - No peer-reviewed results for the EMBOLD EBV T-cell trial (hypothesis 6); no published efficacy results
    for a vaccine that prevents EBV infection (hypothesis 7). A small placebo-controlled trial with
    infection endpoints (NCT05683834) is completed with no results posted, and larger mRNA trials are
    under way (see [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md)).
  - **Antiviral MS trials** (hypothesis 6) now have files: acyclovir (Lycke 1996), valacyclovir
    (Bech 2002, Friedman 2005) and the Höllsberg 2005 EBV and HHV-6B DNA substudy, listed in
    [questions/ebv.md](../questions/ebv.md). None establishes an EBV-mediated benefit, and the trials were not designed specifically
    around EBV. In the sources checked, no newer
    peer-reviewed EBV-directed antiviral trial with MS clinical efficacy results was identified. The randomized TAF-MS study of tenofovir alafenamide added to natalizumab has a congress
    abstract (P1409, MSToronto 2026, *Multiple Sclerosis Journal* 2026;32(4 suppl):559-560; the
    supplement is Crossref-registered as [doi:10.1177/13524585261477876](https://doi.org/10.1177/13524585261477876)
    for the whole poster book, and the single abstract was not read here). As reported by Dotliver
    Tree (bot): 50 people were randomized and 46 completed six months; the reported EBV-activity
    measures were not significantly reduced; safety was the primary outcome, and no relapse or
    disability efficacy was reported.
  - Late harms after aHSCT beyond 100 days are thinly reported, and the aHSCT registry studies may
    share patients (hypothesis 1).
  - Almost all numbers in the evidence files come from abstracts.

## What would change this

- Results from RAM-MS or BEAT-MS: a clear advantage for aHSCT over current high-efficacy drugs
  would strengthen hypothesis 1; no advantage would sharply weaken it.
- A second placebo-controlled trial of a BTK inhibitor in progressive MS, peer-reviewed FENtrepid
  results, or any trial adding a BTK inhibitor to an anti-CD20 drug, would move hypothesis 2 up or
  down.
- A randomized trial in which a repair drug improves a function people notice would move
  hypothesis 4 to the top tier; one in which a myelin measure improves without function would
  weaken it further.
- Any randomized CAR-T result with disability outcomes, any controlled antiviral trial with MS
  outcomes, or an EBV vaccine that prevents infection would change the scores for hypotheses 5 to 7.
