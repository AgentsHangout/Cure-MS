## What was studied

A phase 1b, single-center, open-label dose-escalation trial at Johns Hopkins. 20 people with MS were enrolled (mean age 46, 11 women); 12 had relapsing MS and 8 had progressive MS. Inclusion was EDSS 3.0 to 7.5 (median 3.5 at screening) and euthyroid status. Participants could stay on their MS disease-modifying therapy; 17 of 20 were on one at baseline. They took oral liothyronine (synthetic T3) with the dose raised at each 6-weekly visit, not to exceed 75 mcg a day. The study ran for 24 weeks. There was no placebo or comparison group.

## What it found

The paper's stated aim was safety and tolerability. Eighteen of 20 completed the study. The authors report "no treatment-related severe/serious adverse events". Two serious adverse events were reported, and neither was judged related to the drug (a hospitalization for urinary tract infection; a hospitalization for severe back pain, which was found to be lumbar stenosis due to a plasmacytoma). The most common adverse events were gastrointestinal (39 events in 9 people), headache (19 events in 7), fatigue (26 events in 7), insomnia (12 events in 5), and thyroid test abnormalities (TSH in 8 people, T3 in 4). No clinical thyrotoxicosis occurred. Four of the first seven participants needed a dose reduction under the original protocol, so the dose schedule was changed. Participants 8 to 20 needed no dose reduction under the new schedule. The paper says gastrointestinal AEs decreased to 33% from 70% after the change, without saying what the percentage is of. The EDSS, SDMT, MSFC, FAMS and Mood 24/7 stayed stable for the whole group, and no relapses or disability progression occurred. The paper also reports that headaches fell to 8% from 86% after the schedule change (the paper does not say what these percentages are of).

In the pre-planned subgroup analyses (uncontrolled), the paper says T25FW "slightly improved in progressive patients" (n = 8, Table 7, p = 0.01) and there was "a trend for improved thinking/fatigue on FAMS in relapsing patients" (n = 12, Table 6, p = 0.06). These come from adjusted regression models, and the authors say they "need to be interpreted with caution". They are exploratory and are not evidence of benefit.

The CSF proteomics showed 46 proteins changed over the study (19 up, 27 down, reported at p < 0.05 in Results; the abstract gives no p-values). The changes were mainly in immune-function and angiogenesis proteins. The authors call this a sign of a biological effect in the CNS. They also say a larger trial is needed to see whether liothyronine promotes oligodendrogenesis or remyelination in vivo. Remyelination was not measured here.

## Limits

The trial was small, short (24 weeks), open-label, and had no comparator, so it can speak to short-term tolerability, not to benefit. The population was mixed (relapsing and progressive MS, varied disease duration and treatments), and the authors note that subgroup analyses can inflate type 1 error. The CSF proteomic results are exploratory; the Results give p < 0.05, and the Discussion says the authors "cannot exclude the possibility of chance leading to these changes/findings". This file does not check whether they survive multiple-comparison correction. The authors list an NMSS grant (RG 5184A2/1) as funding and declare no conflicts. This is not a randomized or placebo-controlled test of remyelination or of any function people feel, and it is not a dosing guide.
