# ASTIMS: stem cell transplant vs mitoxantrone in severe relapsing or secondary progressive MS

- **Citation:** Mancardi GL, Sormani MP, Gualandi F, et al. Autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in multiple sclerosis: a phase II trial. *Neurology*, 2015;84(10):981-988.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000001329](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000001329), PMID [25672923](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25672923/)
- **Evidence level:** 2
- **Question:** [questions/cell-therapy.md](../questions/cell-therapy.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A multicenter, phase 2 randomized trial (ASTIMS, EudraCT 2007-000064-24) in people with secondary
progressive or relapsing-remitting MS whose disability (EDSS) had worsened in the past year despite
conventional treatment and who had at least one gadolinium-enhancing (active) lesion on MRI. They
were randomly assigned to:

- intense immunosuppression followed by autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplant (aHSCT):
  mobilization with cyclophosphamide and filgrastim, then conditioning with carmustine, cytosine
  arabinoside, etoposide, melphalan and antithymocyte globulin, or
- mitoxantrone, 20 mg every month for 6 months.

The main outcome was the total number of new T2 lesions on MRI over the 4 years after randomization.
Secondary outcomes were gadolinium-enhancing lesions, relapse rate and disability progression; safety
was also assessed.

## What it found

- In a trial of 21 people (17 with MRI scans that could be evaluated after baseline), aHSCT was
  followed by 79% fewer new T2 lesions than mitoxantrone (rate ratio 0.21, P = 0.00016).
- aHSCT also reduced gadolinium-enhancing lesions and the yearly relapse rate.
- No difference in disability progression was found between the groups.
- The authors conclude that aHSCT was superior to mitoxantrone in reducing MRI activity in severe MS
  and call for phase 3 trials with clinical main outcomes.

## Limits

- Very small: 21 people randomized and 17 evaluable for the main outcome, so it could not reliably
  detect a difference in disability.
- The main outcome is an MRI measure, not disability or relapses.
- The comparison drug, mitoxantrone, is rarely used today. The trial says nothing about how aHSCT
  compares with current high-efficacy drugs such as anti-CD20 antibodies.
- It mixed relapsing-remitting and secondary progressive MS, and used a more intense conditioning
  regimen than the MIST trial ([2019-ahsct-vs-drugs-trial-burt](2019-ahsct-vs-drugs-trial-burt.md)),
  so the two trials are not directly comparable.
- The abstract gives no safety figures, does not describe blinding, and does not describe funding.
  All numbers here come from the abstract (checked on PubMed and Crossref); the full text was not
  available through PubMed Central.
