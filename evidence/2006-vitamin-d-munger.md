# Blood vitamin D levels before MS onset in US military personnel

- **Citation:** Munger KL, Levin LI, Hollis BW, Howard NS, Ascherio A. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels and risk of multiple sclerosis. *JAMA*, 2006;296(23):2832-2838.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1001/jama.296.23.2832](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.296.23.2832), PMID [17179460](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17179460/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (nested case-control study; observational)
- **Question:** [questions/clustering.md](../questions/clustering.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A nested case-control study among more than 7 million US military personnel with serum stored in
the Department of Defense Serum Repository. 257 people with MS (identified for 1992-2004 and
confirmed by medical record review) were each matched to 2 controls by age, sex, race/ethnicity and
blood collection dates. Vitamin D status was the average 25-hydroxyvitamin D level of 2 or more
samples taken before the first MS symptoms.

## What it found

- Among white participants (148 cases, 296 controls), higher vitamin D levels went with lower MS
  risk: odds ratio 0.59 (95% CI 0.36-0.97) for each 50 nmol/L higher level.
- Compared with the lowest fifth (below 63.3 nmol/L), only the highest fifth (above 99.1 nmol/L)
  differed significantly: odds ratio 0.38 (95% CI 0.19-0.75).
- The link was especially strong for levels measured before age 20.
- Among Black and Hispanic participants (109 cases, 218 controls), who had lower levels, no
  significant association was found.

## Limits

- Association, not cause. Vitamin D levels reflect sun exposure, body size, diet and other factors,
  any of which could explain part of the link.
- Modest numbers within each group, and the finding in white participants was not seen in Black and
  Hispanic participants.
- Military personnel are not representative of the whole population.
- This study did not test vitamin D supplements and says nothing about whether taking them changes
  risk.
