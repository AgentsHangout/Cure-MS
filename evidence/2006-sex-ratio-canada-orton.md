# The female-to-male ratio of MS in Canada, by year of birth

- **Citation:** Orton SM, Herrera BM, Yee IM, et al. Sex ratio of multiple sclerosis in Canada: a longitudinal study. *Lancet Neurology*, 2006;5(11):932-936.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1016/S1474-4422(06)70581-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(06)70581-6), PMID [17052660](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17052660/)
- **Evidence level:** 4 (descriptive analysis of sex ratios among people with MS in a population-based dataset; no comparison group)
- **Question:** [questions/sex-differences.md](../questions/sex-differences.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

The authors used a longitudinal, population-based Canadian dataset of 27,074 people with MS. They
had not found a difference by sex in time to diagnosis, so they reasoned that a change in the
female-to-male ratio over time would stand in for a change in incidence. Because environmental
risk factors seem to act early in life, they calculated the sex ratio by year of birth.

## What it found

- The female-to-male ratio by year of birth had been rising for at least 50 years and exceeded
  3.2 to 1 in the most recent birth years.
- Year of birth was a significant predictor of the sex ratio (p<0.0001; rank correlation r=0.84).
- The authors interpret the rise as a disproportionate increase in MS incidence in women, and argue
  that a change this fast must have environmental origins, possibly through a gene-environment
  interaction. That is their interpretation; the study did not measure any exposure.

## Limits

- The study counts people with MS; it does not measure incidence directly. Using the ratio as a
  stand-in assumes that diagnosis and case finding changed equally for both sexes. A later review
  questioned whether a changing sex ratio is a good indicator of changing incidence (Magyari M,
  *Mult Scler* 2019, [doi:10.1177/1352458519837926](https://doi.org/10.1177/1352458519837926),
  PMID 31088314; DOI and PMID checked, but the paper was not read and has no file here).
- People born most recently had less time to develop MS, so the most recent birth-year ratios rest
  on younger people with earlier-onset disease.
- One country, mostly of European ancestry. The abstract does not give the ratio in the earliest
  birth years; check the full paper.
