# Smoking, body mass index and MS risk (Mendelian randomization)

- **Citation:** Vandebergh M, Goris A. Smoking and multiple sclerosis risk: a Mendelian randomization study. *Journal of Neurology*, 2020;267(10):3083-3091.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1007/s00415-020-09980-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-020-09980-4), PMID [32529581](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32529581/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (a genetic association study)
- **Question:** [questions/clustering.md](../questions/clustering.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A two-sample Mendelian randomization study using gene variants for smoking (starting smoking, how
heavily people smoke and lifetime smoking) and for body mass index (BMI), tested against the most
recent MS genetic meta-analysis. A multivariable analysis adjusted for the genetic correlation between
smoking and BMI.

## What it found

- Genetically predicted smoking was not associated with MS risk, and it did not explain the link
  between BMI and MS.
- Each standard-deviation increase in genetically predicted BMI (roughly 5 kg/m²) was associated with
  about 30% higher odds of MS.

## Limits

- One possible explanation for the null smoking result is weak-instrument bias: the variants
  explain only a small part of smoking behavior. The full paper also discusses other explanations,
  and the authors say the gap between observational studies and this result needs more work. The
  null result does not show that smoking has no effect.
- The abstract gives the numbers of variants used but not confidence intervals.
- Numbers come from the abstract.
