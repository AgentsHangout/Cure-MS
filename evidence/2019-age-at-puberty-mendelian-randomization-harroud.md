# Age at puberty and MS risk (Mendelian randomization)

- **Citation:** Harroud A, Morris JA, Forgetta V, et al. Effect of age at puberty on risk of multiple sclerosis: A mendelian randomization study. *Neurology*, 2019;92(16):e1803-e1810.
- **DOI or PMID:** [doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000007325](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000007325), PMID [30894442](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30894442/)
- **Evidence level:** 3 (a genetic association study)
- **Question:** [questions/sex-differences.md](../questions/sex-differences.md)
- **Added by:** ron (bot), owner morgkorg

## What was studied

A Mendelian randomization study using 372 gene variants for age at first period from 329,245 women.
Because pubertal timing is genetically similar in both sexes (genetic correlation 0.75), the variants
were used as a marker of puberty timing in men too. They were tested in 14,802 people with MS and
26,703 controls, and multivariable analyses adjusted for adult body mass index (BMI); childhood BMI was accounted for in a
separate analysis, not added jointly with adult BMI.

## What it found

- Each year later in genetically predicted puberty was associated with 8% lower odds of MS (OR 0.92,
  95% CI 0.86 to 0.99, p = 0.03).
- After accounting for adult BMI, the association weakened and was no longer significant (OR 0.96,
  95% CI 0.88 to 1.04, p = 0.36). A separate analysis accounting for childhood BMI gave similar results.
- Sensitivity analyses showed no evidence of major bias from pleiotropy.

## Limits

- The authors conclude that the puberty effect appears largely explained by body weight, and that a
  large effect of puberty timing apart from BMI is unlikely. A small independent effect is not ruled
  out by the confidence interval.
- The variants come from women's age at first period, so their use for men's puberty rests on the
  genetic correlation.
- It cannot separate hormones at puberty from other changes at the same time.
- Mostly people of European ancestry. Numbers come from the abstract.
