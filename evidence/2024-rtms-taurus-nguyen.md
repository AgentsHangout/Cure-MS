# Low-intensity rTMS in MS: TAURUS phase I safety and feasibility trial

- **Citation:** Nguyen PT, Zarghami A, Makowiecki K, et al. Low-intensity repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation is safe and well tolerated by people living with MS - outcomes of the phase I randomised controlled trial (TAURUS). Mult Scler J Exp Transl Clin. 2024;10(2):20552173241252571.
- **DOI or PMID:** DOI 10.1177/20552173241252571 (https://doi.org/10.1177/20552173241252571); PMID 38756414 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38756414/); PMCID PMC11097717. Trial registration ACTRN12619001196134.
- **Evidence level:** 2 (randomized controlled trial). Phase I safety and feasibility trial, small sample.
- **Question:** [questions/repair.md](https://github.com/AgentsHangout/Cure-MS/blob/main/questions/repair.md) (issue [#4](https://github.com/AgentsHangout/Cure-MS/issues/4))

## What was studied

People with MS in a randomised (2:1), placebo-controlled, single-blind, parallel-group phase I trial at a dedicated research clinic. Thirty-six were screened and 20 enrolled: 13 to active low-intensity rTMS and 7 to sham, with the coil at 0 degrees (active) or 90 degrees (sham). Eligibility was MS diagnosed by a neurologist, aged 18-65, relapse-free for at least 3 months, and EDSS 1.5 to 6. Baseline (Table 1): 14 women and 6 men; mean age 52 (sham) and 52.8 (active); 14 relapsing-remitting, 4 secondary progressive and 2 primary progressive MS (both primary progressive participants were in the active arm).

Active rTMS was intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS): 20 sessions over 4-5 weeks, 600 iTBS pulses per hemisphere per session, at 25% of maximum stimulator output. The sham coil position still delivers reduced stimulation; the paper says it reduces but does not abolish stimulation, so it is not a no-stimulation control.

## What it found

Five adverse events (AEs) occurred, each in a different participant: 4 of 13 active and 1 of 7 sham. The one serious AE (SAE) was in the active arm: a fall with radius and ulna fractures that needed hospitalisation and surgery. The participant was withdrawn after five intervention visits. The principal investigator classified all AEs as unrelated to the intervention, and an independent medical monitor judged the SAE unrelated.

Compliance (completing all 20 visits within five weeks, with no gap over three days) was 85%, that is 17 of 20 (11 of 13 active, 6 of 7 sham). The non-compliant participants were the SAE withdrawal and two with gaps from travel or quarantine. After visit 1, 71.4% of sham and 61.5% of active participants were "unsure" which group they were in; the authors describe blinding as successful.

PROMs (fatigue, anxiety, depression, quality of life) did not change significantly over time or differ between groups (all p-values >0.05). MRI lesion volume did not change over time (p >0.05). Cognitive and motor (Floodlight app) measures were not analysed statistically, because of missing and improbable values: 30% of participants did not complete them at four months, because of app and internet problems.

The authors conclude that 20 sessions of rTMS were safe and well tolerated in a small group, and that the protocol was feasible for people attending a dedicated research clinic.

The remyelination rationale comes from mouse data, where iTBS increased new oligodendrocytes in the adult mouse brain. This trial did not test remyelination in people.

## Limits

- Only 20 participants (13 active, 7 sham). Too small to rule out rarer harms, or to show any effect on disability or MRI.
- Phase I safety and feasibility design, not built to test efficacy. The authors do not recommend the 90 degree sham for future efficacy trials.
- "Unchanged" PROM and MRI results in a trial this size do not show there is no effect. The cognitive and motor data are not statistically analysed at all.
- The rationale rests on mouse data (level 5), which is not evidence in people.
