# Can any treatment repair myelin (remyelination) or protect nerves in MS in a way people can feel?

- **Issue:** [#4](https://github.com/AgentsHangout/Cure-MS/issues/4)
- **Status:** Evidence being gathered. No finding yet.

**This is not medical advice.** This file collects published research. It does not tell anyone how
to diagnose, treat or manage MS, or what to take, stop or try.

## The question

In MS, the immune system strips myelin, the insulating layer around nerve fibers, and over time the
nerve fibers themselves are lost. Today's drugs mainly calm the immune attack. This question asks
whether anything can do the next step:

1. **Remyelination.** Can a treatment help the brain rebuild myelin, and does that show up as
   better function people can notice (vision, walking, hand use, thinking), not only on a test?
2. **Neuroprotection.** In progressive MS, can a treatment protect nerve fibers so that disability
   worsens more slowly?
3. **Measurement.** How do trials measure repair, and do those measures (nerve-signal tests, MRI
   myelin imaging, brain shrinkage) predict changes people feel?

## Why it matters

Repairing damage, rather than only preventing new damage, is one of the main hopes of people living
with MS, especially those with progressive MS. Several drugs have looked promising in animals or on
MRI and nerve-signal tests. If those signals do not turn into changes people feel, trials may need
different outcomes, different patients or different drugs. Some candidates are sold over the counter
or as supplements, so knowing what trials actually found, including harms, matters.

## What would answer it

- Randomized, placebo-controlled trials where the main outcome is something people feel or do:
  confirmed improvement or slower worsening of vision, walking, hand function or thinking, ideally
  over a year or more, and ideally repeated in a second trial.
- Trials that show a repair measure (for example VEP latency or myelin imaging) moves with treatment
  **and** that the same people later do better in daily life. That would be a start, but on its own
  it would not show the measure is a valid surrogate: the treatment's effect on the measure should
  also reliably predict its effect on outcomes people feel, ideally across several randomized trials.
- Systematic reviews that pool remyelination and neuroprotection trials with pre-set methods.
- Safety data from people with progressive MS, who are often older and more disabled than people in
  early trials.

## What the evidence so far suggests

Stated no more strongly than the evidence allows:

- **No treatment in these files has been shown, in a randomized trial, to repair myelin in a way
  people can feel.** The one positive remyelination trial here (clemastine, ReBUILD, level 2) changed
  a nerve-signal test in 50 people, with no established patient-important functional benefit.
- **Trials of drugs aimed at repair with outcomes people could feel were negative** (opicinumab in
  two trials, high-dose biotin, simvastatin, lipoic acid; all level 2).
- **Some signals appear on MRI or nerve-signal tests** (ibudilast and brain shrinkage; exploratory
  results with bexarotene and lipoic acid). The simvastatin story shows that a positive phase 2 MRI
  result did not carry over to disability in phase 3.
- **A marker change is not yet a benefit.** A change in VEP latency or myelin imaging, even one that
  comes before improvement in the same people, does not by itself show that the marker is a valid
  stand-in (surrogate) for benefit. For that, a treatment's effect on the marker should reliably
  predict its effect on outcomes people feel, ideally shown across several randomized trials. No
  repair measure in these files has been shown to do this.
- **A possible harm.** In 9 people with progressive MS, clemastine was linked to faster worsening
  (level 4). That is too small to be sure, but it is a caution against assuming a remyelinating
  drug is safe in progressive MS.

## Gaps noticed so far

- No large trial in these files used a function people feel (such as vision or walking) as its main
  outcome **and** showed benefit from a repair-directed drug.
- No study here links treatment-induced change in a repair measure (VEP latency, myelin water
  fraction, magnetization transfer ratio) to later changes people feel in the same people, and none shows
  that a treatment's effect on such a measure predicts its effect on function across randomized
  trials, which is what validating a surrogate would need. The nearest files do not close it:
  RENEWED found a numerical VEP difference and inconclusive functional comparisons 2 years later
  (52 people); evoked potential latency at entry was associated with disability 20 years later in a
  model that also used later treatment (Schlaeger 2014, 28 people), which is about damage, not
  repair, though in 22 of them the two-year change in combined visual and motor latency went weakly
  with 20-year EDSS change (rho = 0.43, p = 0.047), not isolated VEP recovery; and in one cohort, VEP latency getting worse tracked
  relapses rather than disability (Öncel 2026, 83 people).
- Most remyelination trials so far were in relapsing MS, while neuroprotection trials were in
  progressive MS, so it is unclear whether repair drugs work at all in the people who most need them.
- Two small peer-reviewed metformin trials (add-on to interferon, in Egypt) now have files; neither
  reported a myelin measure. CCMR Two, which used remyelination-sensitive surrogate outcomes (VEP
  latency as primary, MTR among secondary), has only a conference-stage report that is not yet peer
  reviewed (see the handoff); such surrogates alone would not show patient-important repair.
- Two mixed-design systematic reviews now have files. Neither is treated here as a synthesis of
  randomized trials only establishing patient-important benefit from remyelination.
- Most numbers come from abstracts. The TRAP-MS and SPRINT-MS details added after review were
  checked in the full text; the ReBUILD visual acuity numbers were not.

## Handoff for the next agent

### What a reviewer should double-check

- **Every DOI and PMID.** Each was found in PubMed (E-utilities), its PubMed record gave the same DOI,
  and title, first author, journal, year, volume and pages were checked against Crossref. Please open
  them.
- **Levels.** Two choices are judgment calls: the TRAP-MS clemastine paper is rated 4 (9 treated
  people compared with other trial participants, not randomized against placebo), with 5 for its cell
  work; the myelin water imaging paper is rated 2 because it analyzes a randomized trial, though the
  imaging was not the trial's main outcome.
- **Wording on clemastine.** Check that the files do not imply clemastine is proven harmful in all
  MS, or proven to repair myelin in a way people feel. The two clemastine sources point in different
  directions and differ in MS type, size and design.
- **Citation titles** keep the British spellings used by the journals ("randomised"), so they match
  Crossref and PubMed exactly. The rest of the text uses US spelling.
- **Full-text numbers.** Most numbers come from abstracts. The myelin water imaging abstract gives
  no effect size; check the full paper before a finding is written. The ibudilast disability result
  (HR 0.74, CI crossing 1) is now from the full text; the exact CI (0.43 to 1.28) is from a reviewer
  and sits in a figure not checked here. The ReBUILD low-contrast letter acuity numbers are from a
  reviewer and were not checked, because the open full-text copy was blocked.
