# Why does MS cluster in families and in places, and what have investigations of reported clusters found?

- **Issue:** not yet opened (question F)
- **Status:** Evidence being gathered. No finding yet.

**This is not medical advice.** This file collects published research. It does not tell anyone how
to diagnose, treat or manage MS, or how to judge anyone's own risk.

## The question

MS is more common in some families than others, and more common in some places than others. People
also sometimes notice several cases in one town, workplace or school and ask whether something
local is to blame. This question asks:

1. How much of the clustering of MS in families comes from shared genes, and how much from a shared
   environment (the home, diet, infections, habits)?
2. Which environmental factors that people often share, such as Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection,
   sunlight and vitamin D, smoking and body size in adolescence, are associated with MS risk?
3. Why does MS vary by place, by latitude and in people who migrate?
4. When a local cluster of MS has been reported and investigated, what did the investigation find?

## Why it matters

Clustering is one of the oldest clues to what causes MS. If families share MS mainly through genes,
that points one way; if through shared exposures, another. Places with more MS can point to
environmental causes, but they can also mislead: a cluster noticed by chance, or more diagnosis in
one area, can look like a local cause. Knowing what past cluster investigations found helps people
and public health agencies weigh new reports.

## What would answer it

- Large population-based family, twin and adoption studies that separate genetic from
  shared-environment effects, ideally repeated in more than one country and ancestry.
- Prospective cohort studies that measure exposures (such as infection, vitamin D, smoking and body
  size) before MS begins, and studies of how these exposures combine with risk genes.
- Studies that use genetic variants as natural experiments (Mendelian randomization) or trials, to
  move from association toward cause where that is possible.
- Migrant studies and small-area studies that use the whole population and pre-set methods, rather
  than starting from a noticed cluster.
- Systematic reviews of investigations of reported clusters, including ones that found no excess.

### Genetic tests of cause (Mendelian randomization)

These use gene variants that affect an exposure as a natural experiment. They are less open to
confounding and reverse causation than ordinary observational studies, but they rest on assumptions
(no other path from the variants to MS), so they are rated 3.

## What the evidence so far suggests

Stated no more strongly than the evidence allows:

- **Families.** Family, twin and adoption studies (level 3) point mostly to shared genes as the
  reason MS runs in families. The Swedish estimate of shared family environment was close to zero,
  but with a confidence interval up to 0.18, so a modest effect is not ruled out.
- **Environment.** Several exposures that people in a family or a place often share are associated
  with MS risk in level 3 studies: EBV infection (the strongest link), lower vitamin D and less
  UV-B exposure, smoking, and obesity in adolescence, which in one study combined with HLA risk
  genes. These are associations; none of these studies alone shows cause. Mendelian randomization studies
  (level 3) support a causal role for lower vitamin D and higher adult body mass index, but did not
  confirm one for smoking, possibly because smoking variants are weak instruments. Neither kind of
  study shows that raising vitamin D or losing weight in adulthood prevents MS.
- **Places.** MS is more common farther from the equator, and migrants partly take on the risk of
  their new home (level 4, comparisons of places).
- **Reported clusters.** The one reported-cluster investigation here found a statistical excess
  but could not show a cause (level 4). A systematic small-area study found that MS is spread
  fairly evenly, which the authors read as pointing away from local causes (level 4).

## Gaps noticed so far

- No systematic review of reported MS cluster investigations was found. Most published cluster
  reports are small and start from a noticed group of cases, which makes chance hard to rule out.
- Investigations that found **no** excess are probably under-published. None with an abstract was
  found in this search.
- The adoption study abstract gives no counts of adoptees or cases, and the IMSGC abstract does not
  say how much heritability the variants explain. Both need checking in the full papers.
- Mendelian randomization now has files for vitamin D, body mass index and smoking. None tests an
  intervention, and no randomized trial here shows that vitamin D supplements or weight loss prevent
  MS.
- Most of the evidence is from people of European ancestry in high-income countries.

## Handoff for the next agent

### What a reviewer should double-check

- **Every DOI and PMID.** Each was checked by the author bot (see each file), but please open them.
- **Levels.** Two choices are judgment calls: the Hammond migrant study is rated 4 because its main
  findings are prevalence comparisons, even though it includes a case-control analysis (level 3);
  the Handel meta-analysis is rated 3 because it pools observational studies, not trials.
- **Wording on EBV.** The Bjornevik authors suggest EBV is the leading cause of MS. The evidence
  file reports that as their interpretation and words the result as an association. Check this
  balance.
- **Heritability.** Check that the Westerlind file explains heritability as a population statistic
  and does not imply a share of any one person's MS.
- **The Illinois cluster.** Check that the file does not suggest zinc or metals caused MS; the paper
  shows they occurred in the same place and time. A letter on the paper with an author reply exists
  (*Arch Environ Health* 2002;57(4):383) and has not been read.
- **Full-text numbers.** All numbers come from abstracts. Where a file says the abstract lacks a
  number, the full paper should be checked before any finding is written.

### Most useful searches still to do

- Reported cluster investigations with abstracts, especially ones that found no excess. Candidates
  seen in PubMed but not yet given files: Page EH et al., an evaluation of an MS cluster among nurses
  on a hospital ward (*J Occup Environ Hyg* 2015, PMID 25835645, verified but has no abstract in
  PubMed, so no numbers could be taken); Riise T et al. on clustering of where people with MS lived
  at ages 13 to 20 in Hordaland, Norway (*Am J Epidemiol* 1991, PMID 1851394, verified); Nicoletti A
  et al. on a possible cluster in a town in Sicily (*J Neurol* 2005, PMID 15789132, not verified);
  Hammas K et al. on small-area MS incidence in western France (*Int J Health Geogr* 2017, PMID
  28934989, not verified).
- The Faroe Islands MS "epidemics" literature (Kurtzke and colleagues), the best-known reported
  cluster in time and place, and later critiques of it.
- US state health department or CDC/ATSDR cluster investigations of MS, which may not be in PubMed.
- Half-sibling and conjugal (spouse) studies, which separate shared home from shared genes in other
  ways (for example, Ebers GC et al. on conjugal MS, *Ann Neurol* 2000, PMID 11117550, which lacks a
  DOI in PubMed and was not verified).
- Twin studies beyond Sweden, such as the Canadian twin study (Willer CJ et al., *PNAS* 2003, PMID
  14569025) and the Danish twin study (Hansen T et al., *Mult Scler* 2005, PMID 16193885); found in
  PubMed but not verified on Crossref or reviewed.
- Randomized trials of vitamin D supplementation for MS prevention or early MS, to test what the
  Mendelian randomization studies suggest.
- Studies on how EBV, HLA risk genes and other exposures combine, which could explain why risk
  clusters in families that share both genes and some exposures.
