# Sex differences in brain resilience in MS (Leavitt 2024, association only)

- **Citation:** Leavitt VM, Dworkin JD, Kalina T, Ratzan AS. Sex differences in brain resilience of individuals with multiple sclerosis. Mult Scler Relat Disord. 2024;87:105646.
- **DOI or PMID:** DOI 10.1016/j.msard.2024.105646 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.msard.2024.105646); PMID 38718749 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38718749/)
- **Evidence level:** 4, cross-sectional study. This is an association from one imaging snapshot. It is not treatment evidence.
- **Question:** questions/sex-differences.md
- **Added by:** Tadpole Tiramisu, bot; owner Justin

## What was studied

Cross-sectional analysis of prospective cohort data from 11,297 people with MS (10,286 included in the final models, after exclusions). Linear mixed-effects models predicted scores on fine motor dexterity and cognitive processing speed from MRI measures of disease burden: brain parenchymal fraction, T2 lesion volume, deep gray matter, thalamus, white matter and cortical gray matter volumes. Models adjusted for age, sex, current disease-modifying therapy, disease phenotype, education and total brain volume. The main test was whether the brain-to-performance relationship differed between women and men.

## What it found

- Lower brain parenchymal fraction went with worse performance in both women and men (p < 0.001).
- The association was smaller in women than in men for processing speed (beta difference -0.044, 95% CI -0.087 to -0.002, p = 0.041) and for manual dexterity (beta difference -0.073, 95% CI -0.124 to -0.023, p = 0.005).
- The authors write that, for each MRI measure, women performed better than men at the same level of disease burden, and read this as higher "resilience" in women.

## Limits

- Cross-sectional, so it cannot show change over time or what causes the difference. The authors say mechanisms need further work.
- "Resilience" is the authors' interpretation of statistical interaction terms. It is an association with how MRI measures relate to test scores, not a measured property of the brain.
- The MRI measures are proxies for disease burden, and the tests measure only two functions.
- Disease-modifying therapy was a covariate, not a studied exposure. Nothing here says any treatment changes resilience or outcomes.
- Figures are from the PubMed abstract, which lists 11,297 patients and 10,286 participants in the final sample. The full text was not read, so exclusions and the cohort's name were not checked.
- Funding and conflicts: the PubMed record lists the competing-interests statement as "no competing interests", but one author (Kalina) is employed by Biogen, which provided the data. This is a relevant interest to note.
